Architecting the Advisor-Grade DSCR
Engine: A Blueprint for Institutional-
Grade Real Estate Decision Intelligence
Defining the Advisor-Grade Standard: Differentiating
Institutional Rigor from Calculator Simplicity
The transition from a conventional calculator-grade DSCR tool to an advisor-grade
decision engine represents a fundamental shift in purpose, methodology, and output.
While a calculator provides a static number, an advisor-grade engine delivers a dynamic,
auditable, and context-aware assessment of a real estate deal's viability. The highest
standard for an advisor-grade engine is defined by a set of interconnected principles that
collectively elevate it from a simple arithmetic processor to a sophisticated decision
support system. These principles—mathematical precision, underwriting realism, multi-
horizon investor survival analysis, rigorous scenario modeling, granular risk diagnosis,
conservative recommendation logic, unwavering explainability, comprehensive
auditability, embedded compliance, and clear human-review triggers—are not merely
desirable features; they are the pillars upon which institutional-grade trust is built. This
section deconstructs each of these tenets to establish the benchmark for the proposed
engine. The core philosophy is that a deal can be financeable but still economically
unviable, and a deal can be a strong investment without fitting the rigid criteria of a
single lender . The engine’s primary function is to illuminate this distinction, providing
clarity rather than false confidence.
Mathematical precision is the foundation of any credible financial model. An advisor-
grade engine must eschew approximations and black-box algorithms in favor of
deterministic, explicit formulas. Every metric, from Net Operating Income (NOI) to Debt
Yield, must be calculated using a transparent formula where every variable is explicitly
defined and sourced 59 98. For instance, the Debt Service Coverage Ratio (DSCR) itself is
a critical metric, but its definition varies. Fannie Mae defines it as the ratio of Net Cash
Flow to debt service, where Net Cash Flow is derived from NOI after deducting
replacement reserves 90. In contrast, some private lenders may use different definitions,
highlighting the need for a generalized core that can apply multiple definitions based on
the active lender adapter . The engine must version-control every formula to ensure

===== PAGE 2 =====

consistency and traceability, preventing the kind of opaque calculations that regulators
and auditors rightly scrutinize 29 30.
Underwriting realism demands that the engine model the complexities of real-world
property management, not just textbook financial ratios. This means moving beyond a
simplistic assumption of stable, full occupancy and fixed expenses. The engine must
incorporate mechanisms to account for vacancy, delayed tenant placement, unexpected
repairs, fluctuating insurance premiums, and property tax reassessments . Non-QM loans
often rely on manual underwriting to navigate these complexities, and the engine should
reflect this level of scrutiny 45. Furthermore, it must correctly handle various loan
structures, such as interest-only periods, by modeling the full-term amortization schedule
to reveal the true long-term payment obligation, thereby detecting the "interest-only
illusion" 97. This realism is crucial because delinquencies on nonowner-occupied
commercial real estate (CRE) loans have shown significant increases, indicating that
static models fail to capture underlying market stress 128.
Investor survival analysis is arguably the most critical departure from traditional
calculators. It shifts the focus from mere lender eligibility to the long-term economic
viability of the investment itself. This requires a multi-horizon approach that evaluates a
deal across three distinct timeframes: a short-term liquidity runway (0–12 months), a
medium-term stabilized ownership period (13–36 months), and a long-term refinance or
exit horizon (37–60 months) . The 12-month view acts as a liquidity stress test,
determining if the borrower can survive initial costs and potential vacancies before the
property stabilizes 20 22. This concept is borrowed directly from startup finance, where
"runway" measures how long a company can operate before exhausting its cash 41. The
subsequent horizons assess the property's structural resilience against normal market
volatility and its ability to withstand changes in capital markets at the time of refinancing
or sale 123. This aligns with banking guidance that emphasizes the importance of
monitoring liquidity and funding risks throughout a loan's lifecycle 4 36.
Rigorous scenario modeling moves beyond single-point projections to probabilistic
forecasting. The engine must be capable of running a suite of scenarios, including a Base
Case (verified income/expenses), a Conservative Case (moderate negative shocks), a
Severe Case (extreme shocks), and a Cascading Shock scenario (multiple simultaneous
adverse events) . This approach mirrors the macro-financial stress-testing frameworks
used by central banks like the ECB and IMF to assess systemic risk 2 108109. Techniques
such as Monte Carlo simulation can be employed to model the stochastic nature of
variables like rental income and operating expenses, providing a distribution of possible
outcomes rather than a single deterministic value 88102. This allows the user to

===== PAGE 3 =====

understand the probability of success versus failure under various conditions, a far more
powerful insight than a pass/fail box-checking exercise.
Granular risk diagnosis is the engine's diagnostic capability. Instead of simply flagging a
deal as "high-risk," it must identify the specific factors that contribute to that risk. The
engine should categorize risks into distinct domains: income risk (rent decline, vacancy),
expense risk (taxes, insurance, CapEx shocks), leverage risk (LTV, prepayment penalties),
liquidity risk (insufficient reserves), refinance risk (rate shock, market illiquidity), and
data-quality risk (stale or estimated inputs) . By isolating the primary driver of fragility,
the engine provides actionable intelligence. For example, if the main issue is high
vacancy, the solution lies in improving leasing strategy or increasing reserves, not
necessarily in reducing the purchase price. This targeted diagnosis is a hallmark of
professional underwriting and distinguishes the engine from generic screening tools.
Conservative recommendation logic ensures that the engine's output prioritizes investor
safety over lender eligibility. This is achieved through a gated decision hierarchy where a
negative result in one module can block a positive outcome in another . The guiding
principle is that investor survival overrides lender qualification. A deal that passes a
lender's minimum DSCR threshold but fails the investor survival analysis should receive a
recommendation like "Financeable but fragile" or "Qualifies but poor investment," never
"Strong deal" . This logic prevents the promotion of mechanically approved but
economically unsound investments. The recommendation engine must be rule-based,
transparent, and calculation-first, avoiding unsupported advice or claims that could be
misconstrued as personalized financial guidance 79.
Unwavering explainability is non-negotiable for an institutional-grade tool. Every output,
score, and recommendation must be fully traceable back to its source. This means
embedding citations for all external data points (market rents, tax rates), clearly labeling
every user-inputted or estimated assumption, and providing access to the underlying
formulas . The rise of Artificial Intelligence in finance has made explainability a
regulatory imperative, requiring models to be transparent so their decisions can be
understood and challenged 5 8 . Techniques from Explainable AI (XAI) can be used to
quantify the contribution of each input factor to a final score, adding a layer of
interpretability even to complex models 9 . This transparency builds user trust and is
essential for internal governance and external audits.
Comprehensive auditability ensures that the entire analytical process is recorded and
verifiable. The engine must generate a detailed audit trail for every analysis performed.
This log should include all inputs, their source and timestamp, the specific formulas
applied, the version of the lender adapter used, the results of each module, and the

===== PAGE 4 =====

logical path taken through the recommendation engine . This level of detail is consistent
with the OCC's revised Model Risk Management (MRM) guidance, which mandates
robust documentation and record-keeping for all models used by regulated institutions
29 30 91. The audit trail serves as a permanent record of due diligence, protecting both
the user and the provider of the tool.
Embedded compliance boundaries are critical to prevent the engine from overstepping its
role. The system must be explicitly framed as "advisor-grade decision support" and must
not purport to be a licensed mortgage broker, financial advisor, or underwriter unless
supervised by one . It must adhere to regulations like CFPB's Regulation Z, which governs
loan originator compensation and adverse action notices, to avoid creating conflicts of
interest or engaging in prohibited steering 79 89. The engine must have clear triggers for
human review, especially when data is missing or assumptions are highly uncertain,
ensuring that a licensed professional makes the final judgment call . All outputs must
include appropriate disclaimers about the limits of the analysis and the need for
independent verification.
Finally, clear human-review triggers are the ultimate safeguard. The engine is a tool to
augment, not replace, professional judgment. Triggers should be automatically activated
when inputs are missing or stale, when data-confidence scores fall below a certain
threshold, when the investor survival analysis yields weak results, or when the deal falls
into a gray area of uncertainty . When a trigger is engaged, the engine should pause and
present a clear message to the user, such as "Insufficient verified data" or "Requires
human review," along with a summary of the issues identified. This ensures that the tool
operates within its intended scope, empowering users with data while preserving the
irreplaceable role of expert analysis.

Current State Analysis: Deconstructing Core Metrics
and Auditing Existing Tools
A thorough understanding of the current landscape is essential for designing a
breakthrough system. This involves a deep dive into the core financial metrics used in
real estate analysis, benchmarking them against established practices, and then
systematically auditing existing DSCR calculators to map their inherent weaknesses. The
provided materials confirm that while foundational concepts are well-established, their
application in common tools is often superficial and incomplete. This analysis will define
each key metric, explain its purpose and limitations, and then pivot to a critical audit of
basic calculators, revealing the gaps that necessitate a more robust engine architecture.
The vocabulary of real estate finance is built upon a handful of core metrics, each serving
a specific purpose and possessing distinct strengths and weaknesses. Net Operating

===== PAGE 6 =====

Income (NOI) is the cornerstone, representing a property's potential annual earnings
before accounting for financing costs, taxes, and capital expenditures. Its formula is
straightforward:
NOI = Potential Gross Income - Vacancy &amp; Credit Losses + Other Income - Operating Expenses
59. Every component of this formula must be carefully scrutinized. Potential Gross
Income is typically based on market rent, which must be validated against comparable
properties 49. Operating Expenses include everything from property taxes and insurance
to utilities, repairs, and management fees. A common point of manipulation is the
inclusion of a "management fee" in expenses; some owners pay themselves this fee,
inflating expenses and lowering taxable income, but it does not represent a true cash
outflow. A robust engine must differentiate between cash-flow-reducing expenses and
non-cash charges. The primary weakness of NOI is that it is a before-depreciation
measure and does not account for debt service or capital needs, making it an incomplete
picture of overall profitability.
From NOI, several critical ratios are derived. The Debt Service Coverage Ratio (DSCR) is
arguably the most important metric for lenders evaluating income-producing properties.
It is calculated as DSCR=NOI/AnnualDebtService 59. Most commercial lenders
require a minimum DSCR of at least 1.20x or 1.25x, meaning the property's net income
must be 20-25% greater than its annual debt payments 31 32. However, this metric has
significant limitations. It is a static, point-in-time calculation that assumes stable income
and expenses, failing to model the impact of vacancies, rent declines, or expense inflation
over the life of the loan . Furthermore, different lenders define "NOI" differently; some
allow for higher debt service coverage ratios (DSCR) than others, depending on the
property type and location 96. The primary risk is that a property can meet the minimum
DSCR threshold yet still be unable to withstand real-world operational shocks.
Another key ratio is the Debt Yield, calculated as DebtYield=NOI/LoanAmount 98.
Unlike DSCR, which looks forward at cash flow, Debt Yield looks backward at the
relationship between current income and the total loan amount. It is less sensitive to
interest rate fluctuations and provides a more direct measure of the lender's cushion
against a decline in property value. Lenders increasingly use it as a complementary risk
metric alongside DSCR, with balance-weighted debt yields being reported by firms like
CRED iQ 98. Its strength is its simplicity and stability, but its weakness is that it ignores
the actual debt service burden, which could be substantial even if the loan-to-value ratio
is low.
For investors, the Cash-on-Cash Return
(Cash−on−Cash=Pre−TaxCashFlow/TotalCashInvested) and the Capitalization Rate

===== PAGE 7 =====

(CapRate=NOI/PropertyValue) are vital 59. The Cap Rate is a measure of the
unleveraged rate of return on a property, allowing for comparison across different assets.
A lower cap rate implies a higher property value for a given NOI. Both metrics are useful
for initial screening but are insufficient on their own. They do not account for the timing
of cash flows, financing costs, or future market cycles.
More nuanced metrics provide deeper insights. The Break-Even Occupancy (BEO) ratio,
calculated as
BEO=(AnnualOperatingExpenses+AnnualDebtService)/PotentialRentIncome,
indicates the percentage of units that must be occupied to cover all operating and
financing costs 59. It helps investors understand the minimum performance required to
avoid negative cash flow. Expense Ratios, such as the ratio of total operating expenses to
gross income, help normalize spending across properties and identify outliers.
Replacement Reserves are funds set aside to cover major repairs and replacements (e.g.,
roof, HVAC), and their inclusion in the DSCR calculation (as seen in Fannie Mae's
definition of Net Cash Flow) is a critical indicator of long-term maintenance health 90.
Finally, metrics like the Liquidity Runway, measured in months, calculate how long the
available cash reserves would last if the property were to generate zero net income,
providing a direct measure of downside protection 20 22.

This failure map demonstrates that the problems with basic DSCR calculators are
systemic. They are not merely inaccurate; they are fundamentally incomplete. They
provide a single, static snapshot that offers a false sense of security. A property that
qualifies for a loan today might be insolvent in six months due to a vacancy spike or an
insurance premium increase. A deal that meets one lender's requirements might be
unacceptable to another due to differing risk appetites. A simple calculator cannot
capture these realities. The proposed engine architecture directly confronts these failures
by introducing layers of stress testing, data validation, scenario analysis, and
transparency that are absent in conventional tools. It moves the user from a position of
passive calculation to one of active, informed risk management. By building upon the

validated metrics of the industry and correcting their flawed application, the engine can
deliver the factual, calculation-first, and auditable analysis demanded by the research
goal.
Competing Architectures: Designing Seven Distinct
Algorithmic Engines
To innovate beyond incremental improvement, it is necessary to design and evaluate
multiple competing algorithmic architectures. Each proposed engine addresses a specific
facet of the real estate analysis problem, from lender eligibility to investor survival and
risk diagnosis. This modular approach allows for the selection and combination of the
most robust and effective components into a final, synthesized master architecture. The
seven proposed engines—Conservative Underwriting, Investor Survival, Scenario
Simulation, Breakpoint Optimization, DSCR Fragility, Assumption Confidence, and
Advisor Recommendation—each possess unique innovations and potential dangers if
implemented poorly. A rigorous examination of their core ideas, inputs, outputs, and
logic is essential to guide the synthesis process.
1. Conservative Underwriting Engine This engine is a lender-first model focused
exclusively on eligibility. Its core idea is to rigorously evaluate a deal against a specific set
of lender underwriting guidelines to determine if it "qualifies" for financing. It is the first
gate in the decision-making process.
• Inputs: Property details (type, location, age), loan specifics (amount, term),
borrower profile (FICO, debt-to-income), and property financials (NOI, rent roll).
• Outputs: Pass/Fail eligibility status, target DSCR/LTV, qualifying rate/points range,
and a list of reasons for failure (e.g., "Property Type Not Permitted," "FICO Below
Minimum").
• Formulas & Rules: This engine is primarily rule-based, applying strict thresholds
from lender guides or program matrices . For example, it checks if the calculated
DSCR meets the lender's minimum (e.g., 1.25x 32), if the LTV is within the allowed
limit (e.g., up to 80% for purchase at FICO 740+ 96), and if the property type is
acceptable. It also applies overlays for interest-only periods, prepayment penalties,
and reserve requirements.
• Innovation: Its innovation lies in its strict adherence to lender-specific rules,
facilitated by a plug-in adapter system. It separates the question of "Can I get a
loan?" from "Should I invest in this property?" .

===== PAGE 10 =====

• Danger: If poorly implemented, it can become brittle and overly rigid. Hard-coding
too many lender-specific exceptions can make the engine difficult to maintain. It
risks promoting deals that technically qualify but are fundamentally poor
investments.
2. Investor Survival Engine This engine takes a property-first perspective, modeling
whether the investment can survive real-world ownership stress over a multi-year
horizon. It answers the question: "Will this property generate sufficient cash flow to cover
all obligations?"
• Inputs: Detailed pro-forma with monthly breakdowns of income and expenses,
historical performance data, market trends (vacancy, rent growth), and borrower
liquidity.
• Outputs: A series of cash flow statements over time, a calculated Liquidity Runway
(e.g., 18 months), and a qualitative rating (e.g., Strong, Acceptable, Weak, Fail).
• Formulas & Rules: It runs a multi-period cash flow model, incorporating monthly
rent collection, operating expense accruals, and debt service payments. It uses the
concept of a "runway" from startup finance, where the formula is
Runway=TotalCashReserves/AverageMonthlyNetCashFlow 20 22. The engine
simulates the property's performance over a 36-month stabilization period,
factoring in expected vacancies, tax increases, insurance hikes, and planned CapEx .
• Innovation: The innovation is the integration of a true multi-year, cash-flow-based
survival analysis that accounts for operational realities, not just a static DSCR
calculation .
• Danger: Poor estimation of expenses, vacancies, or future CapEx can lead to an
overly optimistic survival rating. The engine is only as good as the quality of its
input assumptions.
3. Scenario Simulation Engine This engine tests the deal's resilience by running it
through a variety of predefined stress scenarios. It answers: "What happens if the market
turns?"
• Inputs: The base-case financial model from the Investor Survival Engine.
• Outputs: Performance metrics (DSCR, cash flow, liquidity) for each scenario,
highlighting the deal's breaking points.
• Formulas & Rules: It modifies the base-case inputs according to scenario
parameters. For a "Severe Case" scenario, it might apply a -15% rent decline, a 25%
insurance increase, and a 20% tax reassessment shock simultaneously . It then re-
runs the cash flow model to see how the deal performs under these combined
pressures.

===== PAGE 11 =====

• Innovation: It elevates the analysis from a single projection to a probabilistic
assessment of risk, mirroring methodologies used by central banks for solvency
stress tests 2 70. The use of cascading shocks is particularly valuable for
diagnosing fragility .
• Danger: The choice of scenarios can be subjective. If the severe scenarios are
unrealistically extreme, the engine may flag every deal as fragile. Conversely, if
they are too mild, the analysis provides little value. The scenarios must be
calibrated to be challenging but plausible.
4. Breakpoint Optimization Engine This reverse-solver answers the practical question:
"What specific change is needed to make this deal workable?" It is a powerful tool for
deal restructuring.
• Inputs: The base-case financial model and the target threshold the deal must meet
(e.g., DSCR >= 1.25x, Liquidity Runway >= 12 months).
• Outputs: The exact dollar or percentage change required in a specific variable to
meet the target. Examples: "Rent must increase by
250/month,′′′′Purchasepricemustdecreaseby50,000," or "Reserves must increase
by $15,000."
• Formulas & Rules: It uses algebraic solving techniques to invert the core financial
formulas. To find the required rent, it would solve the DSCR formula for the rent
variable:
Required_Rent=(DSCRtarget×Annual_Debt_Service)+Operating_Expenses+Va.cancy_Losses−Other_Income
It can perform this calculation for any input variable.
• Innovation: It transforms the engine from a passive analyzer into an active
problem-solver, providing mathematically justified steps for repairing a flawed
deal .
• Danger: It assumes linear relationships, which may not hold in reality (e.g., cutting
price by $50k may not actually increase rent). It can suggest impractical solutions
(e.g., "increase rent by 50%"), which would require further investigation.
5. DSCR Fragility Engine This engine quantifies how easily the deal breaks when key
assumptions move slightly. It answers: "Which risk is the most dangerous for this specific
deal?"
• Inputs: The base-case financial model and the target DSCR threshold.
• Outputs: A "DSCR Fragility Score" and a ranked list of risks by their potential
impact on the DSCR. For example: "1. Rent Decline (-5%), 2. Vacancy Increase
(+5%), 3. Insurance Hike (+15%)".
• Formulas & Rules: It systematically applies small, predefined shocks to each key
input variable (e.g., -5% to rent, +10% to property taxes) and calculates the

===== PAGE 12 =====

resulting DSCR. The fragility is measured by the size of the shock that pushes the
DSCR below the critical threshold. The formula is implicit in the process:
FragilityRent=ShockRent_to_fail.
• Innovation: It provides a quantitative measure of sensitivity, moving beyond
qualitative warnings to a ranked list of threats .
• Danger: The size of the shocks must be carefully calibrated. An arbitrary 10% rent
decline might be reasonable for a Class A office tower but catastrophic for a single-
family rental. The shocks should be contextualized based on property type and
market.
6. Assumption Confidence Engine This engine is a data quality auditor. It answers:
"How much should I trust the numbers in this analysis?"
• Inputs: All user-provided, imported, or calculated data points.
• Outputs: A confidence label for each input (e.g., Verified, User-Provided,
Estimated, Stale, Missing, Assumed) and an aggregate "Assumption Confidence
Score" for the overall analysis.
• Formulas & Rules: This is a rule-based engine that tags each input based on its
source and recency. "Verified" comes from a cited source (e.g., a signed lease
agreement). "User-Provided" is a direct entry. "Estimated" is a calculation based on
limited data. "Stale" is data older than a defined period (e.g., 12 months). "Missing"
is an empty field. "Assumed" is a placeholder value for an unknown.
• Innovation: It forces transparency regarding data quality, which is a critical but
often overlooked aspect of financial modeling. It creates an immediate red flag for
users to investigate weak inputs .
• Danger: The rules for classifying data are subjective and require careful definition.
However, this is a minor risk compared to the danger of proceeding with an
analysis built on unverified, stale, or missing data.
7. Advisor Recommendation Engine This is the synthesis engine, converting the outputs
of the previous six modules into a final, conservative, rule-based recommendation.
• Inputs: The results from the Underwriting, Survival, Scenario, Fragility, and
Confidence engines.
• Outputs: A clear, text-based recommendation (e.g., "Strong Deal," "Financeable but
Fragile," "Avoid Unless Terms Improve").
• Formulas & Rules: It implements a gated decision hierarchy. The core rule is:
Investor Survival Overrides Lender Qualification . The logic is structured as
follows:
• If Assumption Confidence Score is Low -> "Insufficient Verified Data."
• Else if Lender Eligibility is Fail -> "Does Not Qualify for Financing."

===== PAGE 13 =====

• Else if Investor Survival is Fail -> "Qualifies but Poor Investment."
• Else if Investor Survival is Weak -> "Financeable but Fragile."
• Else -> "Strong Deal."
• Innovation: It codifies professional judgment into a logical, transparent, and
conservative framework. It prevents the blending of disparate signals into a
meaningless average score .
• Danger: The rules are complex and must be meticulously defined and validated.
The logic must be robust enough to handle all possible combinations of inputs
without producing illogical or contradictory recommendations.
By designing these seven competing architectures, we create a rich palette of capabilities.
The subsequent phase of adversarial validation will stress-test each one individually
before synthesizing their strongest elements into a unified, powerful, and reliable master
engine.
Synthesized Master Architecture: Integrating
Robustness, Explainability, and Governance
The culmination of the research and design phases is the synthesis of the strongest
components from the seven competing architectures into a single, cohesive, and powerful
master architecture. This unified system is not merely a collection of features but a
logically integrated workflow that embodies the principles of institutional-grade
governance: differentiation, multi-horizon stress testing, gated decision logic, and
unwavering auditability. The final engine is structured as an eleven-module system,
designed for maximum flexibility and robustness. It features a generalized institutional
core that can be adapted to any lender's specific rules via a modular adapter system,
ensuring longevity and relevance in a dynamic market . This architecture is the definitive
answer to the research goal, delivering an implementation-ready blueprint for a true
advisor-grade DSCR Decision Engine.
The engine's structure is deliberately hierarchical and layered, guiding the user from raw
data input through a series of progressively more complex and insightful analyses to a
final, conservative recommendation. This modular design ensures that each component
can be developed, tested, and maintained independently while contributing to a holistic
whole. The architecture is built to be calculation-first, ensuring that all outputs are
grounded in explicit formulas and verifiable inputs, with every step of the process
documented for full auditability . The system is designed to be compliant by default, with

===== PAGE 14 =====

built-in safeguards that prevent it from acting as an unlicensed advisor and trigger
human review when necessary 79.
Below is a detailed description of each of the eleven modules that comprise the master
architecture:
1. Data Intake and Verification Layer: This is the foundation of the entire engine. Its
sole purpose is to capture every piece of data required for the analysis and
immediately classify its quality and source. It does not perform any calculations
itself. It assigns a confidence score to each input using the categories defined in the
Assumption Confidence Engine: Verified (cited source), User-Provided (direct
entry), Estimated (calculated from limited data), Stale (older than a defined
threshold), Missing (empty field), or Assumed (placeholder) . It timestamps every
input and stores its original source. This module generates the critical audit trail
that precedes every analysis, ensuring complete transparency from the outset.
2. Deterministic Formula Engine: This module contains the core library of all
financial formulas used by the engine. It is a read-only repository of explicit,
version-controlled mathematics. It houses the formulas for DSCR, NOI, Cap Rate,
Cash-on-Cash Return, Debt Yield, and all other calculated metrics 59 98. It is
designed to prevent any form of AI-driven interpretation or invention of math,
ensuring that all calculations are deterministic and replicable. Any change to a
formula must be versioned and tracked, a requirement echoed in formal model-risk
management frameworks 29 91.
3. Lender Qualification Engine: This module acts as the first gatekeeper for
financing. It ingests the verified data and runs it through a set of rules specific to a
chosen lender. Powered by the Lender Guideline Adapter, it evaluates the deal
against the lender's proprietary matrix or official guide for DSCR thresholds, LTV
limits, FICO requirements, property type restrictions, prepayment penalty rules, and
interest-only terms . Its output is a clear Pass/Fail eligibility result and a
corresponding pricing quote, if applicable. This module's separation from the core
engine logic is paramount, as it allows the engine to compare a single deal against
dozens of potential financing sources simultaneously.
4. Investor Survival Engine: This module is the heart of the engine's analytical
power. It moves beyond lender eligibility to assess the property's long-term
viability. It runs a multi-period cash flow simulation over a 36-month stabilization
horizon, tracking the property's performance under base-case conditions . It
incorporates a detailed monthly breakdown of income and expenses, models the

===== PAGE 15 =====

impact of expected vacancies and tax increases, and tracks the depletion of liquidity
reserves. Its key output is a projected Liquidity Survival Clock, which translates
the remaining cash reserves into a tangible time estimate (e.g., "18 months of
runway"), directly addressing the owner's most fundamental question: "How long
can I survive if the rent stops coming in?" 20.
5. Pricing and Sensitivity Engine: This module empowers the user to explore the
trade-offs inherent in financing a deal. It allows for the comparison of different loan
scenarios (e.g., higher interest rate vs. more points, lower LTV vs. higher price). It
calculates the impact of these choices on the annual debt service, monthly
payments, and ultimately, the DSCR and cash flow . This functionality is essential
for negotiating with lenders and optimizing the financial structure of the
investment.
6. Scenario and Stress-Test Engine: This module executes the multi-scenario
simulations designed to test the deal's resilience. It runs the base case and then
applies a series of predefined shocks. The Conservative Case might include a 10%
rent decline and a 15% insurance increase . The Severe Case would apply more
extreme shocks, such as a 20% rent drop combined with a tax reassessment and a
major CapEx event, to diagnose the deal's fragility . The engine's output is a
comparative dashboard showing the deal's performance metrics (DSCR, cash flow,
liquidity) under each scenario, visually highlighting the breaking points.
7. Breakpoint and Deal-Repair Engine: This is the engine's problem-solving module.
It functions as a reverse-solver, answering the question, "What specific change will
fix this deal?" . Based on the user's desired target (e.g., achieve a DSCR of 1.25x), it
calculates the exact numerical change required in a key variable. It can determine
the precise rent increase, purchase price reduction, reserve fund addition, or
interest rate improvement needed to bring a failing deal into compliance. This
provides actionable, mathematically justified advice for restructuring a deal.
8. Risk Diagnosis Engine: After identifying that a deal is risky, this module diagnoses
the root cause. It analyzes the results from the Scenario Engine and ranks the
primary threats to the investment. It categorizes risks into distinct types: Income
Risk (from rent decline/vacancy shocks), Expense Risk (from tax/insurance/repair
shocks), Leverage Risk (from high LTV or prepayment penalties), Liquidity Risk
(from an insufficient runway), and Refinance Risk (from potential rate shocks at
maturity) . This granular diagnosis is invaluable for developing a targeted
mitigation strategy.

9. Borrower Suitability Engine: This module introduces the human element into the
analysis. It compares the deal's projected cash flow and risk profile against a set of
user-defined investor goals. Inputs include the investor's target cash flow,
acceptable negative cash-flow tolerance, and desired liquidity level. The engine
then produces a suitability score, helping the user determine if the property aligns
with their personal financial objectives and risk appetite. This bridges the gap
between cold, hard numbers and the subjective art of investment selection.
10. Recommendation Engine: This is the final synthesis module, which takes the
outputs from all preceding modules and generates the engine's ultimate verdict. It
operates on the core principle of gated decision logic, where a failure in any
critical module downgrades the final recommendation . The logic is conservative by
design: Investor Survival Overrides Lender Qualification. The possible outputs
are strictly controlled and rule-based, ranging from "Strong Deal" to "Avoid Unless
Terms Improve" . Each recommendation is accompanied by a concise narrative
explaining the calculation basis, key risks, and any required improvements,
ensuring the output is both intelligent and understandable.
11. Audit and Compliance Layer: This is a cross-cutting module that is active
throughout the entire process. It ensures that every output is compliant and
transparent. It automatically generates a complete audit trail, including all inputs,
their confidence levels, the formulas used, the scenario settings, and the logical
path to the final recommendation . It embeds necessary compliance disclaimers and
triggers a human-review alert whenever data is missing, assumptions are stale, or
the deal enters a gray area of uncertainty. This module is the guardian of the
engine's integrity, ensuring it operates as a trusted decision support tool, not an
autonomous financial advisor.
This eleven-module architecture represents a comprehensive and robust framework for a
next-generation DSCR engine. It is designed not just to calculate, but to analyze,
diagnose, simulate, and advise in a manner that is consistent with institutional standards
of rigor, conservatism, and transparency.
