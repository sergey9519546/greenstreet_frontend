Architecting the DSCR Sovereign OS: A
Seven-Week Sprint to Resolve Eight
Critical Gaps in TimesFM 2.5
This report details a systematic applied research and development plan designed to
resolve eight critical technical gaps within a production-grade forecasting architecture
built upon the TimesFM 2.5 foundation model. The objective is to harden this system,
dubbed the 'DSCR Sovereign OS', into an institutional-grade, mathematically rigorous
credit engine capable of withstanding regulatory scrutiny and extreme market volatility
77 95. The architecture utilizes Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) to specialize the base
model for real estate cashflow forecasting, with a focus on enabling accurate Monte Carlo
stress testing for Debt Service Coverage Ratio (DSCR)-based credit decisions 20111. The
proposed work program is structured as a seven-week sprint, progressing logically from
foundational data topology and tokenization alignment to advanced mathematical
stabilization, sophisticated modeling for spatial generalization and regime adaptation,
and finally, production-grade inference optimization. Each phase directly addresses one
or more of the identified gaps, culminating in a comprehensive validation framework and
a final deliverable designed for regulatory defensibility. The solutions proposed are
grounded in established principles of time-series analysis, transformer architecture, and
modern MLOps practices, drawing from a wide range of academic and industrial sources
to ensure both theoretical soundness and practical applicability 18 70 88.
Phase I: Data Topology and Tokenization Alignment
The initial phase of this research sprint is dedicated to establishing a robust and
semantically correct data topology for feeding information into the TimesFM 2.5 model.
This involves resolving three foundational issues: misalignment between the user-defined
context window and the model's internal patching mechanism, improper separation of
covariate channels, and the flawed handling of missing data points, colloquially known as
"dark months." These gaps, if unaddressed, can silently corrupt the attention mechanisms
and degrade the model's ability to learn meaningful temporal dependencies, irrespective
of the underlying mathematics or model architecture. The primary goal of this phase is to

===== PAGE 2 =====

implement a strict data contract that adheres to the expectations of the TimesFM 2.0+
framework, ensuring that every piece of information provided to the model is correctly
formatted, masked, and interpreted 18 88. Success will be measured through a series of
targeted ablation studies comparing model performance against a baseline configuration
that does not incorporate these fixes. The core hypothesis driving this phase is that
meticulous attention to data topology is a prerequisite for unlocking the full potential of
the foundation model and preventing subtle but critical errors from propagating through
the entire forecasting pipeline.
The first critical issue, Gap 2: TimesFM Patching & Context Length Mismatch, arises
from the fundamental design of TimesFM, which employs a patch-based tokenizer similar
to PatchTST 7 35. The model operates on fixed-size patches of time-series data, and its
attention mechanism is designed to operate within this patched representation. If the
specified context_len for a forecasting task (e.g., 24 months of historical rent data) is
smaller than the model's default patch_len (which is often 32 or 64), the input tensor
will be heavily zero-padded at the beginning to meet the minimum patch requirement 1
2 . This excessive padding dilutes the effective signal-to-noise ratio of the context
window. The attention mechanism, which is theoretically supposed to focus on salient
temporal features, will instead be forced to process a large number of meaningless
padded values, leading to a degradation of model accuracy and potentially unstable
training dynamics 28. The proposed upgrade is to enforce a strict alignment between the
context_len and the model's internal patch size. This can be achieved in two ways:
either by adjusting the user-specified context_len to be a direct multiple of the patch
size (e.g., using 32, 48, or 64 months of history) or by modifying the TimesFM
configuration file in HuggingFace to use a smaller patch_len that better matches the
available data granularity (e.g., setting patch_len=12 to capture annual seasonality)
1 . Critically, any implementation must include proper attention masking. The dataset
loader must generate an attention_mask tensor where positions corresponding to
valid data are marked as 1 and padded positions are marked as 0. This explicit mask
informs the Transformer to ignore the padded regions entirely, preventing it from
treating a padded month as a month with zero rent, which would catastrophically
mislead the model 7 . An empirical study found that masking out patches consisting
entirely of padding values is an effective strategy for mitigating the negative impact of
padding 7 . To evaluate this change, an ablation experiment will be conducted by
training four distinct LoRA variants of the model, each with a different context_len:
24, 32, 48, and 64 months. The performance of each variant will be benchmarked against
the other on a held-out validation set. The primary success metric will be the Continuous
Ranked Probability Score (CRPS), a proper scoring rule for evaluating probabilistic
forecasts. A lower CRPS indicates a better-calibrated and more accurate predictive

===== PAGE 3 =====

distribution. Additionally, monitoring the entropy of the attention weights can provide a
secondary insight; a well-aligned model should exhibit sharper, more focused attention
patterns (lower entropy) compared to a diluted one 66.
The second foundational issue, Gap 3: Covariate Routing (Past vs. Future Channels),
stems from architectural updates in TimesFM 2.0 and later versions. These models have
adopted a stricter, channel-aware design that mandates separate input tensors for past
and future covariates 18 88. The blueprint's approach of stacking all covariates into a
single tuple violates this schema and will lead to silent corruption of the model's cross-
attention mechanism. The model's encoder processes the past_covariates to build a
memory of the historical context, while the decoder uses both the encoded past and the
future_covariates to autoregressively generate predictions. Mixing these two types
of information into one tensor disrupts this carefully orchestrated flow of information,
likely introducing subtle lookahead bias where the model inadvertently uses information
from the forecast horizon to predict the present 16. The upgrade requires a complete
overhaul of the custom dataset and collator logic to conform to the dictionary-based
input format expected by TimesFM. Specifically, the __getitem__ method of the
dataset class must return a dictionary containing explicitly separated tensors for
past_covariates and future_covariates. The past_covariates tensor
([batch, context_len, num_past_covs]) should contain variables that are
known only up to the end of the context window, such as trailing indicators of
delinquency, historical vacancy rates, and STR occupancy levels 15. Conversely, the
future_covariates tensor ([batch, context_len + horizon_len,
num_future_covs]) can contain variables that are known for both the context and
forecast horizons, including deterministic seasonal features (e.g., sin/cos of the month-of-
year), known SOFR forward curves, scheduled tax hikes, and, most importantly, the
cross-sectional macro anchors that will be discussed in the next phase 1 . An ablation
study will compare two model configurations: Model A, which uses the legacy mixed-
tensor approach, and Model B, which implements the strict dictionary-based routing. The
success of this change will be measured by comparing the out-of-sample Winkler Scores
of the two models. The Winkler score evaluates both the accuracy of the central
prediction and the width of the prediction interval; a significant improvement in
calibration (i.e., a higher percentage of actual observed values falling within their
predicted P10-P90 bands) for Model B would confirm that correct covariate routing leads
to better-calibrated uncertainty estimates 16.
The third and final topological issue, Gap 6: Handling "Dark" Months (Missingness),
addresses the pervasive problem of incomplete data in real property management
systems. It is common for rental properties to have months where no payment is recorded

===== PAGE 4 =====

due to renovation, vacancy, or administrative oversight. Treating these null or 0 values
naively is a critical flaw. The sliding window approach will feed these zeros to the model,
which will interpret them as a catastrophic market crash or a total loss of income,
severely contaminating the training process and producing nonsensical forecasts. The
recommended solution is a sophisticated two-pronged approach that separates the act of
imputation from the communication of uncertainty. First, the raw rent time series should
be preprocessed using a simple yet effective imputation strategy, such as forward-filling,
to maintain continuity in the data stream fed to the model 61. Second, and more
importantly, a binary Missingness Indicator Covariate must be introduced. This is a new
feature vector added to the past_covariates that acts as a flag: it takes the value 1 if
the corresponding rent observation was present and 0 if it was missing or imputed 10 11.
By providing this indicator, the model's attention mechanism is explicitly told when a
data point is unreliable. The desired learned behavior is that during periods flagged as
missing, the model automatically widens its P10-P90 confidence bands, accurately
reflecting the heightened uncertainty associated with those months 9 44. This approach
is superior to simple mean or median imputation because it allows the model to learn a
dynamic relationship between missingness events and forecast uncertainty. To test this,
an experiment will be conducted comparing three imputation strategies for the rent
values: (a) forward-fill (the proposed method), (b) filling with the MSA-level mean rent
for that month, and (c) spline interpolation. The success metric will be the dynamically
learned width of the P10-P90 confidence interval specifically during known periods of
artificial missingness. The forward-fill method combined with the indicator covariate is
expected to produce the most realistic and appropriately widened uncertainty bands,
demonstrating that the model has successfully learned to reason about data quality 43
48.
By systematically addressing these three foundational gaps in Phase I, the DSCR
Sovereign OS will transition from a fragile pipeline susceptible to data-related artifacts to
a robust system built on a solid data contract. The resulting data loading infrastructure
will be aligned with the TimesFM model's internal mechanics, ensuring that the attention
mechanism receives clean, correctly formatted, and semantically meaningful inputs. This
sets the stage for the subsequent phases, which will introduce more complex
mathematical and architectural innovations.

Phase II: Mathematical Rigor and Probabilistic
Calibration
With a stable data topology established, the second phase shifts focus to the core
mathematical and statistical integrity of the forecasting model. This phase is dedicated to
resolving three interconnected challenges: the insidious problem of log-return error
compounding, the logical fallacy of quantile crossing in probabilistic outputs, and the
overarching need for a mathematically sound approach to normalizing non-stationary
financial time series. These issues are paramount for building a credit engine that can be
trusted for high-stakes decision-making and stress testing. The solutions explored here
move beyond heuristic fixes toward principled statistical frameworks that guarantee the
mathematical consistency and reliability of the model's output. The central hypothesis is
that leveraging the model's native capabilities and adopting state-of-the-art techniques for
probabilistic forecasting are essential for transforming the DSCR Sovereign OS from a
standard forecasting tool into a rigorously calibrated financial instrument. This phase will
involve a comparative analysis of different mathematical formulations for the target
variable and the output distribution, using metrics that directly measure the properties
being addressed, such as tail risk and quantile monotonicity.
The first challenge, Gap 1: The "Log-Return" Error Compounding Trap, identifies a
fundamental flaw in a seemingly intuitive forecasting strategy. The initial blueprint
suggests forecasting the logarithmic returns of rent (y_t = log(rent_t /
rent_{t-1})) and then reconstructing the full rent path by exponentiating the
cumulative sum of forecasts 80. While this transformation can stabilize variance over
short horizons, it introduces a severe problem for multi-step forecasting. In quantitative

===== PAGE 6 =====

finance, variance is an additive property. Over a 12-month forecast horizon, the
uncertainty (variance) of the cumulative sum of log-returns grows proportionally with
the horizon. When this increasingly uncertain sum is exponentiated to convert it back to
the original scale of raw rent, the additive growth of variance translates into
multiplicative growth of tail risk 5 . This causes the upper tail of the forecast distribution
(e.g., the P90 quantile) to explode to unrealistic, astronomically high values, while the
lower tail (P10) is crushed towards zero. This phenomenon renders the resulting Monte
Carlo simulations useless for stress testing, as they no longer represent plausible
scenarios but rather statistical artifacts of the compounding process 32. The proposed
upgrade is to abandon manual log-transformations and instead rely on the TimesFM
model's native Reversible Instance Normalization (RevIN) for stabilization 12. RevIN
works by subtracting the instance-specific mean and dividing by the instance-specific
standard deviation for each time series within the batch before it enters the model. After
the model generates its output, an inverse normalization step restores the original scale
14. This process effectively makes the input data stationary for the purpose of learning,
without forcing a global logarithmic scale. This is mathematically superior for preventing
tail explosion over a 12-month horizon because it adapts the normalization parameters
locally to the statistics of the context window, preserving the intrinsic variance structure
of the data 12. However, this recommendation must be nuanced with recent findings. A
comprehensive study revealed that standard RevIN contains several theoretical
contradictions and can be fragile, particularly its reliance on non-robust statistics like the
mean and standard deviation. On datasets with extreme outliers, naive RevIN can
catastrophically fail, leading to massive increases in error 13. Therefore, a truly robust
implementation might require a diagnostic-driven approach to choose the normalization
strategy. Key diagnostics include assessing distribution skewness and the empirical k-
factor (a measure of deviation from normality). Based on these diagnostics, one could
select between sensitive methods like RevIN for relatively clean data or robust
alternatives like R2-IN (a robust version of RevIN) for data with heavy tails or outliers
13. Despite these caveats, for typical real estate rent series, RevIN remains a powerful
and pragmatic choice that avoids the pitfalls of manual transformations. To empirically
validate this, an experiment will compare three target variable transformations: Variant A
(log-returns, as baseline), Variant B (raw rent, relying solely on RevIN), and Variant C
(MSA-detrended rent, calculated as Rent / MSA_Zillow_Rent_Index). The primary
success metric will be the P90/P10 ratio at the 12-month horizon. The log-return method
(Variant A) is expected to show an exploded ratio (e.g., >5.0), whereas Variants B and C
should maintain a much more realistic and bounded variance (e.g., a ratio of 1.2 - 1.5),
demonstrating successful control over tail risk 16.

The second challenge, Gap 5: Quantile Crossing in the Continuous Head, addresses a
logical inconsistency that arises when using independent optimization for each quantile.
Standard practice for generating probabilistic forecasts with deep learning models
involves adding multiple output heads, each trained with the Pinball loss function to
predict a specific quantile (e.g., P10, P50, P90) 60. The Pinball loss is strictly consistent,
meaning it encourages the model to produce an output that minimizes the loss for its
target quantile 60. However, because each head is trained independently, there is no
guarantee that the outputs will be ordered correctly. It is possible, especially for highly
volatile properties or in thin markets, for the model to predict a P90 quantile that is
numerically lower than the P50 quantile (i.e., P90 < P50). Such a result is
mathematically impossible for a true cumulative distribution function and breaks
downstream applications that assume monotonicity, such as Monte Carlo simulations or
the construction of coherent risk measures 47. The assistant proposes two solutions. The
first is a simpler penalty-based approach: augment the overall loss function with a term
that penalizes any crossing, such as Loss + λ * max(0, q_low - q_high), where
q_low and q_high are the predicted quantiles for the lower and higher probability
levels, respectively 47. While straightforward to implement, this method can be brittle
and difficult to tune the hyperparameter λ effectively. The second, preferred approach, is
a more principled solution: replacing the discrete quantile heads with a single
Distributional Head that predicts the parameters of a parametric probability distribution
16. The recommended distribution for this task is the Student's t-distribution, which is
characterized by three parameters: location (μ), scale (σ), and degrees of freedom (ν).
The model's final layer is simply modified to output these three parameters instead of
multiple quantiles 16. Training is then performed by minimizing the Negative Log-
Likelihood (NLL) of the observed data under the predicted Student's t-distribution. This
approach is superior because it guarantees monotonic quantiles by construction; the
quantiles are derived analytically from the fitted distribution parameters, ensuring they
are always in the correct order 32. Furthermore, the Student's t-distribution is
particularly well-suited for financial time series, as its heavy tails (controlled by the
degrees of freedom parameter ν) can naturally accommodate the fat-tailed nature of real
estate returns, which are prone to extreme events 80 82. To evaluate this, an experiment
will compare the "Crossing Rate"—the percentage of forecasts where
q_{\tau_1} &gt; q_{\tau_2} for \tau_1 &lt; \tau_2 —between the baseline
quantile head model and the proposed distributional head model. The target for the
distributional head approach is a crossing rate of exactly 0.0%, demonstrating perfect
monotonicity in the output probabilities 84.
Together, these two solutions form a powerful combination for achieving mathematical
rigor. By choosing a stable normalization scheme like RevIN and a guaranteed-monotonic

===== PAGE 8 =====

probabilistic output format like a Student-T distributional head, the DSCR Sovereign OS
ensures that its forecasts are not only accurate but also logically consistent and
statistically well-behaved. This level of mathematical fidelity is a cornerstone of an
institutional-grade credit engine and is a critical step toward meeting the stringent
requirements of regulators who demand robust model risk management frameworks 95
101. The final validation report for the sovereign OS will need to include detailed
calibration backtests, akin to Hosmer-Lemeshow tests for classification models, to
demonstrate that the predicted quantiles match their empirical coverage probabilities
across the entire forecast distribution 77.
Phase III: Spatial Generalization and Cross-Sectional
Anchoring
Phase III addresses the critical need for the DSCR Sovereign OS to generalize beyond the
idiosyncratic history of an individual property and understand the broader economic
forces that govern real estate markets. This phase focuses on resolving Gap 4: Spatial
Blindspots & Cross-Sectional Anchors, which describes a fundamental limitation of
treating each property as a completely isolated forecasting unit. Without external
guidance, the model learns local patterns from its limited context window but fails to
grasp the concept of "contagion" or shared macroeconomic shocks that affect an entire
Metropolitan Statistical Area (MSA) 4 23. This forces the model to relearn basic
economic gravity for every single property, severely hampering its ability to make reliable
forecasts for new properties in unfamiliar geographies or during novel market-wide
events. The proposed solution is to inject Cross-Sectional Macro Anchors into the
model's input, providing it with a crucial link to the wider economy. This technique
effectively bridges the gap between univariate models that treat each entity
independently and true multivariate models that explicitly capture cross-sectional
dependencies 1 96. The core hypothesis is that by giving the attention mechanism a
strong, consistent signal from the macro-environment, the model can learn transferable
features and achieve vastly improved out-of-sample geographic generalization. The
success of this intervention will be validated through a rigorous Geographic Hold-Out
Test, measuring the reduction in error on geographically unseen MSAs.
The architectural innovation lies in how external information is integrated. Instead of
trying to build a complex, fully coupled multivariate model that feeds all properties' data
simultaneously—a computationally expensive and architecturally challenging endeavor—

===== PAGE 9 =====

the proposed solution is more elegant and efficient 1 . It involves injecting key
macroeconomic indicators for an MSA as a future covariate for every single property
located within that same MSA. The most suitable candidates for these anchors are
publicly available, high-frequency datasets that track local market conditions. The
primary anchor is the MSA-level Zillow Rent Index (ZRI), which provides a normalized,
repeat-sales index of rent changes across the MSA 4 . Secondary anchors could include
the MSA-level vacancy rate or other relevant indices of economic health 23. These
macroeconomic time series are concatenated with the property-specific past and future
covariates and fed into the future_covariates channel of the TimesFM model. This
gives the attention mechanism a clear "macro anchor" to latch onto. During training, the
model learns a powerful correlation: "when the MSA-level ZRI shows a sharp decline, this
specific property's rent trajectory is highly likely to follow suit, even if its own recent history
doesn't yet reflect that trend." This imbues the model with a form of domain knowledge
about real estate market dynamics, allowing it to generalize far more effectively. The
effect is akin to teaching the model the "grammar" of how local markets behave in
response to national or regional economic cycles 1 .
To validate the effectiveness of this approach, a Geographic Hold-Out Test will be
conducted. This is a more stringent evaluation than a simple time-series split. The
procedure is as follows: 1. Data Partitioning: All properties are grouped by their MSA
identifier. A random subset of MSAs (e.g., 10%) is reserved as a hold-out set. The model
is trained exclusively on data from the remaining 90% of MSAs. 2. Training: The model
is trained using the full suite of covariates, including the newly injected MSA-level ZRI
and vacancy rates as future anchors. 3. Validation: The trained model is evaluated on the
hold-out set of MSAs. The forecasts for properties in these unseen MSAs are compared
against their actual rent trajectories. 4. Comparison: The performance of the model with
macro anchors will be compared against a control model that was trained identically but
without the MSA-level covariates.
The primary success metric for this experiment will be the Mean Absolute Percentage
Error (MAPE) on the hold-out MSAs. The hypothesis is that the model with macro
anchors will exhibit a significantly lower MAPE compared to the model without them.
This reduction in error demonstrates that the anchors have successfully transferred
knowledge from familiar markets to unfamiliar ones, improving the model's spatial
generalization capabilities. This is a critical capability for a sovereign AI system intended
to operate across a diverse portfolio of properties in numerous geographies. By anchoring
the local, micro-level predictions to a robust, macro-level signal, the DSCR Sovereign OS
becomes less of a black box memorizing individual property histories and more of a
transparent, reasoning engine grounded in the fundamental economic realities of the

===== PAGE 10 =====

housing market. This approach aligns with the principle of incorporating explanatory
variables to leverage additional information, a practice shown to improve forecasting
performance in various domains, including retail demand forecasting where calendar and
price data are used to great effect 15 16. The integration of these cross-sectional anchors
transforms the model from a collection of parallel univariate predictors into a cohesive
system that understands its place within a larger, interconnected market ecosystem.
Phase IV: Regime-Adaptive Forecasting with Specialized
Adapters
Phase IV confronts the most sophisticated challenge among the eight gaps: Gap 7:
Regime-Blended LoRA (The "Sovereign" Edge). Financial markets, and real estate
markets in particular, are not static; they operate in distinct economic regimes
characterized by different interest rate environments, inflation dynamics, and liquidity
conditions 5 . A monolithic LoRA adapter, trained on a long, continuous period that
spans multiple regimes (e.g., 2015–2025, covering both a low-rate era and a high-rate/
inflationary era), will inevitably struggle. The optimization process will attempt to find a
single set of adapter weights that represents an average of the dynamics from both
regimes. This results in a "mushy middle" that is highly accurate for neither environment.
The model will fail to capture the unique behavioral patterns of either the low-rate or
high-rate periods, leading to poor performance when the current economic conditions
shift. The proposed solution is a paradigm shift in the fine-tuning strategy: instead of
training a single generic adapter, the team will train Multiple Specialized Adapters and
develop a mechanism to dynamically blend them at inference time based on the
prevailing macroeconomic regime 51. This creates a regime-aware model that can adapt
its forecasting behavior to the current economic climate without requiring continuous
retraining, embodying the "sovereign" aspect of the DSCR Sovereign OS by making it
resilient to structural economic changes.
The implementation of this strategy involves a multi-step process. First, the training data
will be partitioned chronologically to isolate the distinct regimes. Two specialized LoRA
adapters will be trained:
• Adapter A (LoRA-Zero): This adapter will be trained exclusively on data from the
Low-Rate Regime, defined as the period from 2015 to early 2022. This data
captures a market environment of historically low interest rates, steady

===== PAGE 11 =====

appreciation, and high liquidity, which influences tenant demand, refinancing
behavior, and property values in specific ways 5 .
• Adapter B (LoRA-One): This adapter will be trained exclusively on data from the
High-Rate/Inflation Regime, covering the period from 2022 to the present. This
data reflects a market characterized by rising interest rates, increased borrowing
costs, potential cooling of demand, and greater economic uncertainty 5 .
Both adapters will be initialized with identical hyperparameters (e.g., rank r=16, alpha
α=32) and will be applied to the same layers of the frozen TimesFM base model (e.g.,
q_proj and v_proj in the last few transformer layers) to ensure they are learning
comparable representations of the underlying dynamics 1 2 . The rationale is that by
training each adapter on a more homogeneous dataset, it can learn the specific patterns
and relationships characteristic of its designated regime without being confused by
contradictory signals from another.
The second, and more innovative, part of the solution is the implementation of a
Dynamic Weight Blending mechanism at inference time. This is the core of the "regime-
aware" capability. When a new forecast is required, the system will not just apply a single
set of adapter weights. Instead, it will compute a final set of weights by interpolating
between the base model weights, the weights of Adapter A, and the weights of Adapter B.
The formula for this is expressed as:
Wfinal=Wbase+γ⋅WZero+(1−γ)⋅WOne
Here, Wbase represents the frozen weights of the pre-trained TimesFM model, while
WZero and WOne are the trained weights of the two specialized adapters. The blending
factor, γ, is a scalar between 0 and 1 that determines the influence of each adapter. The
key question is how to determine γ in real-time. The proposal is to define γ using a
simple heuristic gating function based on a readily available, high-frequency
macroeconomic indicator. For example, a rule could be implemented: if the current 10-
Year Treasury yield is below 3.5%, γ is set to 1.0 (favoring the low-rate regime adapter);
if the SOFR rate is above 4.0%, γ is set to 0.0 (favoring the high-rate regime adapter);
and for values in between, a linear interpolation is used. This approach is inspired by
online adaptation techniques but offers a simpler, more interpretable alternative 8 . The
ADAPT-Z method, for instance, proposes online adjustment of latent feature
representations, which is a more complex adaptive system 8 . For a sovereign AI, the
transparency and determinism of the proposed blending mechanism may be more
valuable for regulatory review than a continuously adapting system whose internal logic
is harder to audit.

The success of this advanced adapter topology will be rigorously tested. The primary
experiment will compare the performance of the blended model against a strong
baseline: a single, monolithic LoRA adapter trained on the entire 2015–2025 dataset.
Both models will be evaluated on an out-of-sample test set comprising vintages from
2023–2024, a period that straddles the regime shift and is therefore challenging for the
monolithic model. The primary success metrics will be the Continuous Ranked Probability
Score (CRPS) and the Pinball Loss, evaluated separately for the P10, P50, and P90
quantiles. The hypothesis is that the blended model will achieve a lower CRPS and
Pinball Loss, indicating a more accurate and better-calibrated forecast. This phase
represents a significant architectural evolution for the DSCR Sovereign OS, moving it
from a static model to a dynamic system capable of navigating the inherent non-
stationarity of financial time series. By creating and intelligently combining specialized
experts, the system gains a crucial form of resilience, a hallmark of a truly sovereign-
grade AI.
Phase V: Inference Optimization for Real-Time
Underwriting
The final phase of the sprint is dedicated to production readiness, focusing squarely on
Gap 8: Excessive Inference Latency. While the preceding phases have been concerned
with the accuracy and mathematical correctness of the model, this phase addresses its
operational viability. Running a 500-million-parameter transformer model through a
native Python implementation via the HuggingFace Trainer is fundamentally too slow
for the intended use case of a real-time underwriting API that must process thousands of
loan applications per minute 6 . The current estimated latency of ~200ms per property
is prohibitive, as it would make interactive, browser-side Monte Carlo simulations with
10,000 paths impossible while a borrower is on the phone 1 . The goal of this phase is to
reduce this latency by a factor of ten or more, targeting a sub-20ms inference time per
property, thereby unlocking the system's full potential for dynamic, real-time decision
support. The solution is a well-established, modern MLOps workflow involving model
serialization, graph optimization, and hardware-specific compilation.
The first step in this optimization pipeline is ONNX Export and Graph Surgery. ONNX
(Open Neural Network Exchange) is an open-standard format for representing machine
learning models. Exporting the finalized, merged TimesFM model (with LoRA weights
incorporated into the base model) to the ONNX format is the critical first step toward

===== PAGE 13 =====

cross-platform deployment and optimization 70. This is accomplished using the
torch.onnx.export function, which traces the model's computation graph and saves
it in a portable format. During this export, it is important to specify dynamic axes for
dimensions that vary at inference time, such as batch size and sequence length, to
maximize flexibility 70. Once the model exists in the ONNX format, a process of "graph
surgery" can be performed. This involves inspecting the ONNX graph and removing any
nodes that were required for training but are unnecessary for inference, such as certain
dropout operations or backward-pass computations related to normalization layers 70.
The goal of this surgical cleanup is to create a leaner, more efficient computational graph
that is tailored specifically for the forward pass.
The second and most impactful step is TensorRT Compilation. NVIDIA TensorRT is a
high-performance deep learning inference optimizer and runtime library. It takes the
serialized ONNX graph and performs a suite of aggressive optimizations tailored to run
on NVIDIA GPUs. These optimizations include: layer and tensor fusion (combining
multiple operations into a single kernel launch to reduce overhead), kernel auto-tuning
(selecting the fastest available implementation for each operation), and precision
calibration. The most significant benefit comes from quantization. TensorRT supports
running models in reduced precisions like FP16 (half-precision floating point) and INT8
(8-bit integer). Converting the model to FP16 can dramatically reduce memory
bandwidth requirements and increase computational throughput on modern GPUs,
typically yielding a latency reduction of over 80% with minimal to no loss in forecast
accuracy 69 70. While INT8 quantization offers even greater potential speedups, its
performance can be hardware-dependent, and in some cases, an INT8 engine compiled
with TensorRT can be slower than its FP16 counterpart, depending on the specific GPU
architecture and the model's sensitivity to quantization error 71. Given the target of
sub-20ms latency, starting with FP16 is the most prudent strategy. The final output of
