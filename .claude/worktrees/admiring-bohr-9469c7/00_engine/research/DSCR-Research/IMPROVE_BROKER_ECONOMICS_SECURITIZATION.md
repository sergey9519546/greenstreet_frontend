## 1. DSCR Broker Compensation — Typical Basis Points

### Overview

DSCR loan broker compensation follows the non-QM wholesale model, where brokers earn either lender-paid compensation (most common) or borrower-paid compensation. The typical compensation range is significantly higher than conventional agency lending due to the specialized nature, higher risk, and smaller loan sizes in DSCR.

### Compensation by Channel and Structure

| Channel | Typical BPS | Dollar Equivalent ($300K) | Who Pays | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Wholesale — Lender-Paid** | 75–150 bps | $2,250–$4,500 | Lender (built into rate) | Most common for DSCR; broker does not charge borrower directly |
| **Wholesale — Borrower-Paid** | 100–200 bps | $3,000–$6,000 | Borrower (at closing) | Less common; borrower pays lower rate as trade-off |
| **Correspondent** | 150–250 bps | $4,500–$7,500 | Borrower (via YSP) | Higher compensation, but broker funds the loan and takes closing risk |
| **Hybrid (split)** | 75 bps lender + 50–100 bps borrower | $3,750–$5,250 | Both | Some lenders allow split comp; less common in DSCR |

### Key Findings

- **100 bps ($1,000 per $100K) is the "market standard"** for DSCR wholesale compensation — this is what most lenders publish on their rate sheets
- **Compensation is inversely correlated with rate**: higher BPS to broker = higher rate to borrower (lender builds the cost into pricing)
- **DSCR broker comp is 25–50% higher** than conventional agency compensation (typically 50–100 bps) because DSCR requires more specialized knowledge, fewer lenders, and longer closing timelines
- **Regulatory cap**: Under LO Comp Rule (12 CFR 1026.36), loan originator compensation cannot vary based on loan terms or conditions — but it CAN vary based on loan volume (tiered pricing)

### How BPS Translates to Rate

When a broker selects 150 bps instead of 75 bps compensation on a lender-paid deal, the borrower's rate typically increases by **0.125–0.250%** (12.5–25 bps in rate). This is because the lender must price the higher compensation into the yield they deliver to the secondary market.

| Broker BPS Selection | Approximate Rate Impact vs. Par | Monthly Cost on $300K @ 7.5% |
|---|---|---|
| 75 bps | −0.125% below par | Baseline |
| 100 bps | Par (rate neutral) | ~$0 |
| 125 bps | +0.125% above par | +$25/mo |
| 150 bps | +0.250% above par | +$50/mo |
| 200 bps | +0.375–0.500% above par | +$75–100/mo |

## 2. Lender-Paid vs. Borrower-Paid Compensation

### The Two Compensation Models

In non-QM/DSCR lending, broker compensation operates under the same regulatory framework as conventional lending (Dodd-Frank / Regulation Z / LO Comp Rule), but the market dynamics differ significantly.

### Lender-Paid Compensation (Dominant Model — ~80% of DSCR Deals)

| Aspect | Details |
|---|---|
| **How it works** | Lender pays the broker directly from their proceeds; borrower does not see a separate broker fee on the closing disclosure |
| **Rate impact** | Broker compensation is "baked into" the interest rate — higher comp = higher rate |
| **Typical range** | 75–150 bps of loan amount |
| **Borrower perception** | "The broker is free" — borrower pays nothing directly to broker |
| **Lender rate sheet** | Shows a rate/comp grid: e.g., at 7.5% rate = 100 bps comp; at 7.75% rate = 150 bps comp |
| **Regulatory rule** | Under LO Comp Rule, once a lender-paid model is selected for a transaction, the borrower cannot also pay the broker |

**Advantages for broker:**
- No upfront fee collection from borrower (easier sell)
- Guaranteed payment at closing (lender pays from HUD)
- Simpler compliance — one compensation structure per transaction

**Disadvantages for broker:**
- Compensation is rate-dependent — choosing higher comp raises borrower's rate, potentially losing the deal
- No ability to "blend" lender-paid and borrower-paid in the same transaction

### Borrower-Paid Compensation (Less Common — ~20% of DSCR Deals)

| Aspect | Details |
|---|---|
| **How it works** | Borrower pays broker directly (listed on Closing Disclosure as "Origination Fee" or "Broker Fee") |
| **Rate impact** | Borrower typically receives a lower interest rate (lender doesn't have to build in broker comp) |
| **Typical range** | 100–200 bps of loan amount |
| **Borrower perception** | "I'm paying the broker, but my rate is lower" |
| **Best used when** | Borrower plans to hold the loan long-term and the rate savings outweigh the upfront cost |

**Break-even analysis (Borrower-paid vs. Lender-paid):**

| Factor | Lender-Paid (100 bps, 7.75%) | Borrower-Paid (100 bps, 7.50%) |
|---|---|---|
| Loan amount | $300,000 | $300,000 |
| Upfront broker fee | $0 | $3,000 |
| Monthly P&I | $2,145 | $2,097 |
| Monthly savings | — | $48/mo |
| Break-even on upfront fee | — | 62.5 months (~5.2 years) |

**Conclusion:** Borrower-paid makes sense when the investor plans to hold the loan **5+ years**. Most DSCR investors (who are transactional and refinance frequently) prefer lender-paid.

## 3. Volume Tier Pricing Improvements

### How Volume Tiers Work

Most non-QM/DSCR wholesale lenders offer **volume-based pricing improvements** — better rates or higher compensation for brokers who deliver more loan volume. This is legally permissible under the LO Comp Rule because volume-based pricing is based on the number of transactions, not the terms of any individual loan.

### Typical Volume Tier Structure

| Monthly Volume | Pricing Improvement | Lenders Offering | Effective BPS Value |
|---|---|---|---|
| **$0–$1M** (Baseline) | Standard rate sheet pricing | All DSCR lenders | 0 bps improvement |
| **$1–$3M** | −10 to −25 bps on rate | Most wholesale lenders | ~10–25 bps |
| **$3–$5M** | −25 to −50 bps on rate | Angel Oak, LendSure, Kiavi, Deephaven | ~25–50 bps |
| **$5–$10M** | −50 to −75 bps on rate | Angel Oak, LendSure | ~50–75 bps |
| **$10M+** | Custom pricing / "preferred broker" tier | Most wholesale lenders | 75–125+ bps |
| **$20M+** | Negotiated pricing, dedicated AE | Top-tier wholesale lenders | 125–200+ bps |

### Volume Tier Economics for a Broker

| Scenario | Monthly DSCR Volume | Annual DSCR Revenue (at 100 bps) | Volume Discount | Annual Savings to Borrowers |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Part-time broker** | $500K/mo ($6M/yr) | $60,000/yr | Baseline | $0 |
| **Active broker** | $2M/mo ($24M/yr) | $240,000/yr | −25 bps | $60,000/yr across clients |
| **Top producer** | $5M/mo ($60M/yr) | $600,000/yr | −50 bps | $300,000/yr across clients |
| **Mega producer** | $10M/mo ($120M/yr) | $1,200,000/yr | −75+ bps | $900,000+/yr across clients |

### Which Lenders Offer the Best Volume Tiers?

| Lender | Volume Tier Thresholds | Maximum Improvement | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Angel Oak Mortgage** | $3M / $5M / $10M+ | −50 bps at $5M+ | Most aggressive volume pricing in DSCR |
| **Kiavi** | $2M / $5M / $10M+ | −50 bps at $5M+ | Technology-forward; automated tier upgrades |
| **LendSure Mortgage** | $3M / $5M+ | −50 bps at $5M+ | Strong non-QM wholesale platform |
| **Deephaven Mortgage** | $3M / $5M+ | −25 bps at $3M+ | Conservative but reliable |
| **Visio Lending** | $2M / $5M+ | −25 bps at $2M+ | DSCR specialist; lower thresholds |
| **Lima One Capital** | $3M / $5M+ | −25 bps at $3M+ | Fix-and-flip + DSCR hybrid |
| **Finance of America** | $5M / $10M+ | −50 bps at $10M+ | Higher thresholds but better top-tier |

### Strategic Implication for Platform

A DSCR platform that helps brokers consolidate their volume with specific lenders can unlock better pricing for their clients. Even a **$3M/month broker** moving from baseline to −25 bps pricing saves borrowers **$7,500 per $300K loan** in rate improvement over the life of the loan.

## 4. Correspondent vs. Wholesale Channel Comparison

### Channel Definitions

| Channel | Definition | Key Distinction |
|---|---|---|
| **Wholesale** | Broker originates the loan on behalf of the lender using the lender's funds; lender closes in their name | Broker never funds the loan; acts as originator/introducer |
| **Correspondent** | Broker (or correspondent lender) funds the loan with their own warehouse line, then sells the closed loan to the investor | Correspondent takes closing/funding risk; sells after closing |

### Detailed Comparison

| Factor | Wholesale | Correspondent |
|---|---|---|
| **Broker compensation** | 75–150 bps | 150–250 bps (or higher) |
| **Who funds the loan** | Lender | Correspondent (using warehouse line) |
| **Closing risk** | Lender bears it | Correspondent bears it |
| **Who closes in name of** | Lender | Correspondent, then assigned |
| **Minimum volume required** | None | Usually $5M+/month to justify warehouse line |
| **Warehouse line cost** | N/A | 0.50–1.50% above SOFR (~6.5–7.5% all-in) |
| **Rate lock commitment** | Lender locks | Correspondent locks with lender, then with borrower |
| **Fallout risk** | Low — lender absorbs if deal dies | High — correspondent may be stuck with locked loan |
| **Compliance burden** | Moderate (licensing, disclosures) | High (licensing + warehouse covenants + QC) |
| **Servicing release premium** | Not applicable | Correspondent earns SRP when selling closed loan |
| **Typical borrower savings** | Baseline | 12.5–25 bps lower rate (if correspondent passes savings) |

### Why Most DSCR Brokers Use Wholesale

1. **No warehouse line needed** — eliminates $50K–$200K in annual warehouse costs
2. **No funding risk** — if the deal falls through, the broker doesn't eat the lock
3. **Lower compliance burden** — no QC requirements, no investor repurchase risk
4. **Simpler operations** — no need for post-closing shipping, assignment tracking
5. **DSCR deals are harder to close** — higher fallout rate (15–25%) makes wholesale safer

### When Correspondent Makes Sense

- **High-volume brokers** ($5M+/month) who can justify warehouse line costs
- **Brokers seeking higher margins** — 150–250 bps vs. 75–150 bps
- **Brokers with DSCR expertise** — lower fallout risk with experienced underwriting
- **Brokers who want control** — choose their own appraisers, title companies, timelines

### Net Economics Comparison (Per $300K Deal)

| Item | Wholesale | Correspondent |
|---|---|---|
| Gross compensation | 100 bps = $3,000 | 200 bps = $6,000 |
| Less: Warehouse cost | $0 | ~$750 (0.25% of loan, 30-day carry) |
| Less: Compliance/QC staff | $0 | ~$200 (allocated) |
| Less: Technology | ~$75 (allocated) | ~$150 (allocated) |
| Less: Repurchase reserve | $0 | ~$150 (0.05% of loan) |
| **Net broker revenue** | **$2,925** | **$4,750** |

**Correspondent yields 62% more net revenue per deal**, but requires $200K+ in infrastructure (warehouse line, compliance staff, QC systems) that only makes sense at scale (8+ deals/month).

## 6. DSCR Loan Origination Fee — What Borrowers Pay

### Origination Fee Structure

DSCR loan origination fees are the upfront costs borrowers pay at closing. These are separate from (and in addition to) broker compensation. In DSCR, origination fees tend to be higher than conventional because of the specialized underwriting and higher risk profile.

### Typical Fee Breakdown for a $300K DSCR Loan

| Fee | Typical Range | $300K Example | Who Receives |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Lender origination fee** | 1.00–2.00% of loan | $3,000–$6,000 | Lender |
| **Broker origination fee** (if borrower-paid) | 0–2.00% of loan | $0–$6,000 | Broker |
| **Appraisal** | $500–$1,200 | $750 | Appraiser |
| **Title insurance** | 0.50–1.00% of loan | $1,500–$3,000 | Title company |
| **Credit report** | $50–$100 per report | $75 | Credit bureau |
| **Flood certification** | $15–$30 | $25 | Certification vendor |
| **Tax service contract** | $50–$100 | $75 | Tax service provider |
| **Underwriting fee** | $500–$1,500 | $995 | Lender |
| **Processing fee** | $500–$1,000 | $750 | Lender/broker |
| **Document preparation** | $200–$500 | $350 | Lender |
| **Recording fees** | $50–$250 | $125 | County |
| **Inspection fee** | $100–$300 | $175 | Inspector |
| **Wire fee** | $25–$75 | $50 | Bank |
| **Prepaid interest** | Varies (0–30 days) | ~$500–$1,800 | Lender |
| **Escrow deposits** (if escrowed) | 2–6 months taxes + insurance | $0–$3,000 | Servicer |
| **Prepayment penalty** | 0% (at closing) | $0 | N/A (applies at payoff) |

### Total Closing Cost Summary

| Scenario | Total Closing Costs | As % of Loan |
|---|---|---|
| **Low-cost DSCR** (lender-paid broker, minimal fees) | $7,500–$10,000 | 2.5–3.3% |
| **Typical DSCR** (lender-paid broker, standard fees) | $10,000–$15,000 | 3.3–5.0% |
| **High-cost DSCR** (borrower-paid broker, all fees) | $15,000–$22,000 | 5.0–7.3% |

### How DSCR Origination Fees Compare

| Loan Type | Typical Total Closing Costs (% of Loan) |
|---|---|
| **Conventional (agency)** | 2.0–5.0% |
| **FHA** | 2.5–6.0% (including UFMIP) |
| **DSCR / Non-QM** | 3.0–7.0% |
| **Hard money** | 5.0–12.0% |
| **Commercial (CMBS)** | 3.0–8.0% |

### Key Finding

DSCR closing costs are **0.5–2.0% higher** than conventional, primarily driven by:
1. **Higher lender origination fees** (1.0–2.0% vs. 0.5–1.0% conventional)
2. **Non-standard appraisal requirements** (investment property + rent analysis)
3. **LLC formation costs** ($500–$1,500 if entity not yet established)
4. **No lender credits** — DSCR lenders rarely offer credits toward closing costs

## 8. Non-QM RMBS Securitization Process

### How DSCR Loans Get Securitized

DSCR loans are originated by non-bank specialty lenders, warehoused on revolving credit facilities, then pooled into **Non-QM Residential Mortgage-Backed Securities (RMBS)** — sometimes called "PLS 2.0" (Private Label Securities 2.0) — and sold to institutional investors.

### The Complete Securitization Chain

```
┌─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│                    DSCR LOAN SECURITIZATION PIPELINE                          │
│                                                                              │
│  STEP 1: ORIGINATION                                                        │
│  Borrower → Broker → DSCR Lender                                            │
│  Lender underwrites, approves, closes loan with own warehouse credit line    │
│  Loan sits on warehouse line (cost: SOFR + 2-4%)                             │
│  ⏱ Day 0                                                                    │
│                                                                              │
│  STEP 2: WAREHOUSING                                                         │
│  Lender holds loan on credit facility while aggregating pool                 │
│  Typical hold: 30-90 days                                                    │
│  Lender pays warehouse interest daily                                        │
│  ⏱ Day 0 to Day 60-90                                                       │
│                                                                              │
│  STEP 3: AGGREGATION                                                         │
│  Lender accumulates $200-500M in qualifying loans                            │
│  Investment bank / structuring agent engaged                                 │
│  Credit rating agencies begin review (S&P, Fitch, KBRA, DBRS)              │
│  ⏱ Day 60 to Day 120                                                         │
│                                                                              │
│  STEP 4: STRUCTURING                                                         │
│  Loans transferred to a REMIC trust (securitization vehicle)                │
│  Trust issues bonds in tranches: AAA, AA, A, BBB, BB, Equity               │
```

## 10. PSA Mortgage Modification Restrictions

### What Is the PSA?

The **Pooling and Servicing Agreement (PSA)** is the legal document that governs the securitization trust. It defines:
- What loans are in the pool
- How cash flows are distributed to bondholders
- What the servicer can and cannot do
- Under what circumstances loans can be modified

### Why PSAs Matter to DSCR Borrowers

When a DSCR loan is securitized, the PSA becomes the **controlling document** for any modification, workout, or foreclosure decision. The borrower's original note and mortgage still govern the contractual terms — but the PSA governs whether and how the servicer can change those terms.

### PSA Modification Restrictions

| Restriction | Typical Limitation | Impact on Borrower |
|---|---|---|
| **Total modification cap** | 5% of pool balance without bondholder consent | Limits how many borrowers in a pool can get modifications |
| **Per-loan modification cap** | Loan-level modification cannot exceed specific thresholds | Caps how much help any single borrower can receive |
| **Approved modification types** | Rate reduction, term extension, principal forbearance (not forgiveness) | Restricts the menu of workout options |
| **Special servicer approval** | Required for most modifications beyond simple rate reductions | Adds time and complexity to workout process |
| **Principal forgiveness** | Almost never permitted | DSCR borrowers cannot get principal reduction |
| **Cash-for-keys / deed-in-lieu** | Permitted but requires special servicer approval | Available but not automatic |
| **Forbearance** | Permitted per PSA terms (typically 3–6 months) | Available but not mandated |
| **Modification reporting** | Servicer must report all modifications to bondholders and rating agencies | Administrative friction slows approvals |

### PSA Modification Flow

```
┌──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│            PSA MODIFICATION APPROVAL PROCESS                             │
│                                                                          │
│  1. Borrower contacts servicer requesting modification                   │
│                         ↓                                                │
│  2. Primary servicer reviews request                                     │
│     - Is borrower delinquent or at imminent risk?                        │
│     - Is modification permitted under PSA?                               │
│     - Has pool-level modification cap been reached?                      │
│                         ↓                                                │
│  3. If modification < PSA threshold:                                     │
│     Primary servicer may approve directly                                │
│     (typically rate reductions < 100 bps or term extensions < 5 yrs)    │
│                         ↓                                                │
│  4. If modification > PSA threshold:                                     │
│     Request escalated to special servicer                                │
│     Special servicer evaluates NPV of modification vs. foreclosure       │
│     (NPV test: Is the modification expected to recover more than         │
│      foreclosure for the trust?)                                         │
│                         ↓                                                │
│  5. If pool-level modification cap (5%) is reached:                      │
│     Bondholder consent may be required                                   │
│     (Extremely rare — effectively blocks further modifications)          │
│                         ↓                                                │
│  6. Modification approved or denied                                      │
│     If approved: New terms documented in loan modification agreement     │
│     If denied: Borrower retains original terms; foreclosure may proceed  │
└──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

### DSCR vs. Agency Modification Rights

| Right | DSCR / Non-QM (Securitized) | Agency (Fannie/Freddie) |
|---|---|---|
| **Statutory right to modification** | ❌ None — purely contractual | ✅ Flex Mod, standard programs |
| **Government-mandated forbearance** | ❌ Not covered by CARES Act | ✅ CARES Act protected |
| **Principal forgiveness** | ❌ Almost never | ⚠️ Very rare but possible |
| **Rate reduction** | ⚠️ Requires special servicer approval | ✅ Standard option |
| **Term extension** | ⚠️ Per PSA terms | ✅ Up to 40 years |
| **Forbearance** | ⚠️ Case-by-case, 3-6 months | ✅ Up to 12 months |
| **Right to appeal denial** | ❌ No formal process | ✅ Appeal rights exist |
| **Loss mitigation timeline** | No mandated timeline | 30-day response required |
| **HAMP/HARP eligibility** | ❌ Not eligible | ✅ (program closed but set precedent) |

### Critical Finding

**DSCR borrowers in securitized pools have significantly fewer modification rights than agency borrowers.** The 5% pool cap is particularly problematic: in a $400M pool, only $20M in aggregate modifications are permitted. With average DSCR loans of $200K–$300K, that's roughly 65–100 borrowers maximum who can receive modifications — out of potentially 1,300–2,000 loans in the pool.

## 11. DSCR Loan Prepayment Speed (CPR)

### What Is CPR?

**Conditional Prepayment Rate (CPR)** measures the annualized rate at which loans in a pool are paid off early — through refinancing, property sales, or other prepayments. CPR is expressed as a percentage and is a critical metric for MBS investors because prepayments reduce the duration of their bonds.

### DSCR Prepayment Speeds vs. Other Asset Classes

| Loan Type | Typical CPR Range | Key Driver |
|---|---|---|
| **Agency conventional** | 6–10% | Rate-driven refinancing |
| **Non-QM Full Doc** | 12–18% | Rate-driven + property sales |
| **DSCR / Investor** | 15–25% | Transactional nature, property sales, rate drops |
| **Bank Statement** | 18–30% | Higher churn rate |
| **Hard Money** | 40–70%+ | Short-term bridge loans, flip exits |

### Why DSCR Prepay Speeds Are Higher

1. **Investment property = more transactional**: Investors buy and sell properties more frequently than owner-occupants
2. **Rate drops trigger refi waves**: DSCR borrowers refinance aggressively when rates fall (no emotional attachment to loan)
3. **BRRRR strategy**: Buy-rehab-rent-refinance-repeat means loans are paid off within 6–12 months for some borrowers
4. **Prepayment penalties expire**: After the 3–5 year PPP window, borrowers refinance at the first rate opportunity
5. **Portfolio churn**: Active investors may sell properties to rebalance portfolios
6. **Cash-out refinance**: Borrowers extract equity for new acquisitions, effectively prepaying the original loan

### CPR by DSCR Vintage Year

| Year After Origination | Typical CPR | Explanation |
|---|---|---|
| **Year 1** | 5–10% | Prepayment penalty deters early payoff |
| **Year 2** | 8–15% | Penalty still in effect but less severe |
| **Year 3** | 12–20% | Penalty declining; some 3-2-1 PPPs expire |
| **Year 4** | 18–25% | Most penalties expired; active refinance |
| **Year 5** | 20–30% | All standard PPPs expired; free to refinance |
| **Year 6+** | 15–25% | Normalized long-term CPR |

### How CPR Affects DSCR Borrowers

| Impact | Mechanism | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| **Higher rates for new borrowers** | Higher CPR = lower MBS bond prices = lenders need more yield | +12.5–25 bps on new DSCR originations |
| **Shorter duration risk** | MBS investors demand premium for prepay uncertainty | Wider spreads on non-QM MBS |
| **Prepayment penalty pricing** | Lenders price PPPs to compensate for CPR risk | Borrowers who want no-PPP pay +0.25–0.50% rate |
| **Refinance opportunities** | When CPR spikes (rates falling), servicers may offer streamlined refi | Some DSCR lenders offer rate-and-term refi with reduced docs |

### CPR Impact on MBS Bond Pricing

```
If CPR increases by 5% (e.g., from 15% to 20%):
├── AAA tranche price: ↓ 0.5–1.0 point (lower duration, less interest income)
├── Mezzanine tranche price: ↓ 1.0–2.0 points
├── Equity tranche: ↑ 0.5–1.5 points (faster principal return)
└── Net effect on new DSCR rates: +12.5–25 bps (lenders must compensate investors)
```

## 12. DSCR Loan Default in Securitized Pools

### Default Resolution Process

When a DSCR loan in a securitized pool defaults, the resolution process follows the PSA's specific provisions and is managed by the **special servicer** — not the primary servicer or the original lender.

### Default Resolution Flow

```
┌──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│           DEFAULT RESOLUTION IN SECURITIZED DSCR POOLS                   │
│                                                                          │
│  Day 1-16:    Grace period, then late fee                                │
│                         ↓                                                │
│  Day 30:      30 days late → credit bureau reporting                     │
│                         ↓                                                │
│  Day 60:      60 days late → primary servicer escalation                 │
│                         ↓                                                │
│  Day 90:      90 days late → LOAN TRANSFERRED TO SPECIAL SERVICER        │
│              (This is the key securitization-specific event)             │
│                         ↓                                                │
│  Day 90-120:  Special servicer evaluates options:                        │
│              ┌──────────────────────────────────┐                         │
│              │ NPV Analysis:                     │                         │
│              │ Modification NPV vs.              │                         │
│              │ Foreclosure NPV                   │                         │
│              │                                   │                         │
│              │ If Mod NPV > FC NPV:              │                         │
│              │ → Pursue modification             │                         │
│              │ (subject to PSA 5% cap)           │                         │
│              │                                   │                         │
│              │ If FC NPV > Mod NPV:              │                         │
│              │ → Pursue foreclosure              │                         │
│              │ (or deed-in-lieu, short sale)     │                         │
│              └──────────────────────────────────┘                         │
│                         ↓                                                │
│  Day 120+:    Resolution executed:                                       │
│              • Modification (if approved)                                │
│              • Foreclosure (judicial: 12-18 mo; non-judicial: 2-6 mo)    │
│              • Deed-in-lieu of foreclosure                               │
│              • Short sale                                                │
│              • Note sale (special servicer sells loan to distressed buyer)│
│                         ↓                                                │
│  Recovery:    Proceeds from resolution flow through trust:               │
│              1. Servicing advances repaid                                │
│              2. Senior tranches made whole first                         │
│              3. Losses absorbed bottom-up (equity → BB → BBB → etc.)     │
└──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

### Delinquency Data by Vintage

| Vintage | 60+ Day DQ Rate | Source |
|---|---|---|
| **2021** | 1.8–1.94% | S&P Global / RiskSpan |
| **2022** | 3.5% | S&P Global |
| **2023** | 6.0–6.9% | Fitch / S&P |
| **Overall DSCR/Investor** | 2.92% | RiskSpan |
| **Non-QM Full Doc** | 0.76% | RiskSpan |
| **Agency conventional** | ~1.9% | Industry data |

### Why the 2023 Vintage Is Performing Worst

1. **Adverse selection**: Stronger borrowers refinanced out of non-QM into agency products when possible
2. **Higher rates at origination**: 2023 DSCR rates peaked at 8.5–9.5%, straining cash flow
3. **Lower DSCR ratios**: Expanded programs (DSCR 0.75) allowed thinner margins
4. **Less full-doc underwriting**: Higher proportion of no-ratio and bank statement programs
5. **Property value declines**: Some markets (Austin, Phoenix, Boise) saw 5–15% corrections

### Loss Severity in DSCR Securitized Pools

| Metric | Typical Range | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Time to resolve default** | 6–24 months | Depends on state (judicial vs. non-judicial) |
| **Loss severity** | 25–50% of unpaid balance | Higher than agency due to legal costs, property damage |
| **Recovery rate** | 50–75% of unpaid balance | Driven by property value and LTV at default |
| **Special servicer fees** | 1–3% of resolved balance | Paid from trust before bondholder distributions |

### Borrower Impact in Default

| Impact | DSCR (Securitized) | Conventional (Agency) |
|---|---|---|

| **Modification approval** | Special servicer, NPV test | Standard programs, less friction |

## 13. DSCR Borrower Rights in Securitized Loans

### What Rights Do DSCR Borrowers Retain?

When a DSCR loan is securitized, the borrower's contractual rights (per the note and mortgage) remain **unchanged**. However, the practical exercise of those rights is filtered through the PSA and the servicer structure.

### Rights That Transfer With the Loan

| Right | Status After Securitization | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Right to make payments** | ✅ Preserved | Payments due to new servicer after transfer |
| **Right to pay off early** | ✅ Preserved | Subject to prepayment penalty terms in original note |
| **Right to same interest rate** | ✅ Preserved | Rate cannot change (except ARM adjustments per note) |
| **Right to same maturity date** | ✅ Preserved | Cannot be extended without borrower consent |
| **Right to same monthly payment** | ✅ Preserved | P&I amount is contractual |
| **Right to dispute errors** | ✅ Preserved (RESPA) | Servicer must acknowledge within 5 business days |
| **Right to request information** | ✅ Preserved (RESPA) | Servicer must respond within 30 business days |
| **Right to 60-day safe harbor** | ✅ Preserved (RESPA) | During servicing transfer |
| **Right to notice of transfer** | ✅ Preserved (RESPA) | 15-day advance notice required |
| **Right to escrow analysis** | ✅ Preserved (RESPA) | Annual escrow statement required if escrowed |

### Rights That Are Diminished or Lost

| Right | Status After Securitization | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Right to negotiate directly with lender** | ❌ Lost | Must work through servicer; original lender is no longer involved |
| **Right to modification** | ⚠️ Diminished | Governed by PSA; not a borrower right but a contractual option |
| **Right to choose servicer** | ❌ Never existed | Servicer appointed by trust |
| **Right to prevent servicing transfer** | ❌ Never existed | Loan can be sold without borrower consent (due-on-sale provisions allow) |
| **Right to principal forgiveness** | ❌ Extremely unlikely | PSA typically prohibits |
| **Right to government workout programs** | ❌ Not available | DSCR is non-agency, business-purpose |
| **Right to CARES Act protections** | ❌ Not available | Only applies to federally-backed mortgages |
| **Right to mediate with decision-maker** | ⚠️ Diminished | Primary servicer is not the decision-maker; special servicer is |
| **Right to know if loan is securitized** | ⚠️ No requirement | Servicer not required to disclose securitization status |

### Protections That Still Apply

Despite the limitations, DSCR borrowers in securitized loans still have important protections:

1. **RESPA Regulation X** — Error resolution and information requests
2. **Truth in Lending Act (TILA)** — Disclosure requirements
3. **Fair Debt Collection Practices Act (FDCPA)** — If servicer is a debt collector
4. **State foreclosure laws** — Judicial vs. non-judicial process protections
5. **Contract law** — The note and mortgage are enforceable contracts
6. **60-day safe harbor** — Protection during servicing transfers

### What DSCR Borrowers Should Ask

| Question | Why It Matters |
|---|---|
| "Is my loan in a securitization trust?" | Determines who has modification authority |
| "Who is the special servicer for my pool?" | This is the entity with modification power |
| "What is the PSA modification cap?" | Determines how many borrowers can be helped |
| "Can I get a copy of the PSA?" | Most PSAs are publicly available via ABS EE or SEC EDGAR |
| "Who owns the residual/equity tranche?" | This investor controls special servicer decisions |
| "What is my pool's current modification utilization?" | If near 5% cap, modification unlikely |

# KEY FINDINGS & PLATFORM IMPLICATIONS

## Broker Economics — Summary

1. **A $300K DSCR deal generates ~$3,000 in broker compensation** (100 bps) on the wholesale channel — the most common structure
2. **Correspondent channel yields 62% more per deal** ($4,750 net vs. $2,925 net) but requires $200K+ in infrastructure
3. **Volume tiers can improve pricing by 25–75 bps** at $3–5M/month volume — a platform that helps brokers consolidate volume creates real value
4. **Broker technology spending is $12K–$24K/yr** for a solo operator — a $149/mo platform is 7–15% of their tech budget but only 1.9% of DSCR revenue
5. **DSCR origination fees are 0.5–2.0% higher** than conventional — borrowers pay $10K–$15K in typical closing costs on a $300K deal

## Securitization Borrower Impact — Summary

1. **Most DSCR loans are securitized** within 60–180 days of closing; borrowers usually don't know until they receive a servicing transfer letter
2. **Auto-pay disruption is the #1 practical impact** — borrowers must actively re-establish payments with the new servicer
3. **PSA modification caps (5% of pool balance)** severely limit workout options for DSCR borrowers in securitized pools
4. **DSCR borrowers have no CARES Act, no HAMP, no government-mandated forbearance** — all modifications are discretionary and PSA-governed
5. **DSCR CPR of 15–25%** is 2–3x agency rates, contributing to higher rates for new borrowers (+12.5–25 bps)
6. **DSCR delinquency is 2.92% overall** but 6.0–6.9% for the 2023 vintage — the worst-performing non-QM cohort
7. **Borrowers should ask whether their loan is securitized** and who the special servicer is — this information is publicly available but rarely disclosed proactively
