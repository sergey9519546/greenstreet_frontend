From Kill Code to Legal Notice:
Architecting the ECOA/FCRA Adverse
Action Engine for Institutional
Compliance
The development of an institutional-grade underwriting platform requires more than
mathematical precision; it demands robust legal defensibility. This report outlines a
comprehensive blueprint for architecting the Explainability_Layer, a critical
component designed to transform the DSCR Underwriting Engine into a tool compliant
with the Equal Credit Opportunity Act (ECOA), Regulation B, and the Fair Credit
Reporting Act (FCRA). As the engine evolves from a risk-assessment calculator to a
decision-making instrument for licensed lenders, its ability to provide clear, specific, and
legally mandated explanations for adverse actions becomes paramount. This layer will
intercept automated denials, map them to standardized regulatory reason codes, and
assemble a complete Adverse_Action_Notice_Payload. The architecture prioritizes
operational flexibility through client-specific activation and configurability, ensuring the
core engine remains adaptable while providing ironclad compliance for regulated
entities. This document details the strategic rationale, architectural design, mapping
methodologies, and technical specifications necessary to build this essential compliance
module.
Strategic Imperative: From Internal Risk Management
to External Regulatory Compliance
The primary strategic driver for developing the Explainability_Layer is the
fundamental shift in the engine's application context. While the DSCR Underwriting
Engine's existing "Kill Criteria" are highly effective for internal risk management, they fall
short of meeting the stringent requirements of federal consumer financial protection laws
19. When a licensed lender or broker utilizes this engine to make an automated credit
decision, every aspect of that decision becomes subject to oversight by regulatory bodies

===== PAGE 2 =====

like the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (CFPB) 16. The current descriptive but
informal reasons for denial, such as "Deal structurally unviable," are insufficient and
expose the lender to significant legal risk 3 10. Noncompliance with the FCRA can lead
to civil and criminal penalties, and the act permits consumers to bring a private right of
action for certain violations, including recovery of costs and reasonable attorneys' fees 4
9 . Therefore, the imperative is to bridge the gap between the engine's granular,
internally-focused risk factors and the standardized, legally-prescribed language
mandated by ECOA and the FCRA. This transformation elevates the engine from a
proprietary risk tool to a defensible compliance asset, enabling its use in regulated
environments without exposing clients to regulatory action.
This transition is not merely about replacing one set of words with another. The
regulations demand specificity and accuracy. Under Regulation B, a creditor's adverse
action notice must contain a statement of reasons that is "specific" and indicates the
"principal reason(s) for the action taken" 8 . These reasons must directly relate to and
accurately describe the factors actually considered by the creditor 8 . The CFPB has
issued guidance, particularly Circular 2023-03, which clarifies that relying on overly
broad statements or selecting the closest identifiable factor on a checklist is insufficient if
it fails to reflect the actual principal reason used by the algorithm 8 . For example,
stating "insufficient projected income" would be inadequate if the model's actual principal
reason was an applicant's chosen profession 8 . This places a heavy burden on the
technology to understand the precise data points and thresholds that triggered a denial
and translate them into legally sound language. The engine must move beyond simple
flagging and embrace a more nuanced understanding of causality within its own logic.
This necessitates enriching the kill events themselves, ensuring they carry not just the
criterion that failed (e.g., LTV > 80%) but also the specific calculated values (e.g.,
actual_LTV = 85.2%, appraised_value = $480,000). Only with this enriched
data can the Explainability_Layer select the most accurate and least ambiguous
regulatory code, thereby fulfilling the spirit and letter of the law. The failure to do so
could result in enforcement actions, as seen in cases where creditors were found to have
violated ECOA by failing to provide adequate adverse action notices 10 19.
Furthermore, the rise of complex credit models and AI in underwriting introduces
additional layers of scrutiny. The CFPB has explicitly stated that using AI or complex
models requires creditors to provide more specific adverse action communications than
codified sample forms allow 8 . This trend toward greater transparency means that the
Explainability_Layer cannot be a static lookup table. It must be a dynamic system
capable of interpreting the outputs of sophisticated algorithms and articulating the key
drivers behind a decision in plain English, tailored to the specific circumstances of the

===== PAGE 3 =====

application 8 . The platform's long-term viability in the institutional space depends on its
ability to evolve alongside these regulatory expectations. By proactively building a robust
and flexible Explainability_Layer, the DSCR Engine positions itself not just as a
calculator, but as a forward-looking compliance platform that mitigates risk for its
licensed clients and builds trust with applicants by providing clear, actionable feedback.
Architectural Design: A Configurable, Client-Specific
Compliance Module
To balance the need for robust compliance with the diverse operational needs of the
platform's user base, the Explainability_Layer must be architected as a standalone,
configurable, and client-specific module. This design choice is central to the project's
success, as it allows the core engine to remain flexible for non-regulated use cases while
delivering full legal protection exclusively where it is contractually and legally required.
The architecture should be gated, meaning the compliance features are activated only for
users who have an active lending license. This licensing check acts as a switch; for non-
licensed users, the engine operates with its existing, more descriptive but less formal
messaging. For licensed clients, this switch is flipped, routing all KILL decisions through
the Explainability_Layer before a final adverse action notice is generated. This
approach prevents non-regulated users, such as passive investors analyzing a portfolio,
from being burdened with a complex compliance interface, while simultaneously
ensuring that any entity acting as a creditor is fully protected against regulatory
challenges. This model aligns with modern software-as-a-service (SaaS) practices, where
functionality is often gated behind licenses or subscription tiers, allowing for a modular
and scalable product offering 6 7 .
The second pillar of the architectural design is configurability. The mapping between the
engine's internal kill criteria and the external regulatory reason codes is not a static
relationship. Lender overlays, evolving regulatory interpretations, and potential state-
level variations necessitate a dynamic system that can be updated without requiring a full
code deployment or a release cycle. The mapping logic should therefore be externalized
into a dedicated configuration file or database, perhaps structured as a YAML or JSON
file tied to the client's profile 14. This configuration would define the rules for translating
each internal trigger into one or more ECOA/Reg B reason codes. For instance, a single
rule might specify that an LTV exceeding 90% maps to ECOA Code 27 ("Collateral value
is insufficient"), while an LTV between 80% and 90% maps to Code 26 ("Maximum loan

===== PAGE 4 =====

amount is exceeded"). This allows compliance teams at individual lender clients to fine-
tune the mappings to match their specific underwriting overlays without developer
intervention. The broader engine could ship with a default, federally-compliant
configuration, which clients could then customize. This approach mirrors the successful
strategy employed for other dynamic components of the engine, such as LLPA grids, tax
tables, and stress scenarios, all of which are driven by external configurations 6 7 . The
Explainability_Layer should also be designed to be extensible, with hooks to easily
add new regulatory requirements or update disclosure language as laws change, ensuring
the platform remains compliant over time.
Finally, the module's architecture must be resilient and well-defined in its inputs and
outputs. The layer should sit logically between the engine's core decision logic and its
final output rendering. Its input is a standard "kill event" object. Critically, this object
must be enriched with contextual data beyond just the failing criterion. It should include
the raw user inputs, the engine's calculated intermediate values (e.g., LTV, DSCR,
Breakeven_Occupancy), and the specific threshold that was breached. This enriched
data is the fuel for the layer's intelligent mapping process. The layer's output is a
structured Adverse_Action_Notice_Payload. This payload is a comprehensive,
machine-readable object (e.g., JSON or XML) that contains all the necessary components
for a legally compliant notice. This includes the standard ECOA prohibition notice, the
array of selected reason codes, specific textual explanations for each reason, required
FCRA disclosures for third-party data, and any other notices mandated by configured
state laws. By defining this clear contract—a richly-enriched input object and a structured
payload output—the Explainability_Layer becomes a self-contained, reusable, and
testable service. This modularity simplifies testing and maintenance, as the core engine's
risk logic and the compliance layer's legal logic can be tested and updated independently,
reducing the risk of regression and ensuring both components function correctly in
isolation and together.
Mapping Internal Kill Criteria to Federal Reason Codes:
A Nuanced Approach
The heart of the Explainability_Layer lies in its ability to accurately map the DSCR
engine's internal kill criteria to the standardized reason codes prescribed by ECOA and
Regulation B. A simplistic, one-to-one mapping where each kill criterion corresponds to a
single, generic reason is legally insufficient. The CFPB's guidance makes it clear that
creditors must provide reasons that are "specific" and identify the "principal reason(s) for

===== PAGE 5 =====

the action taken" 8 . This mandates a more nuanced, dynamic approach that considers
the specific data values associated with a kill event to select the most precise and least
ambiguous regulatory code available. The goal is to move from generic statements like
"Insufficient Income" to descriptions that are grounded in the specific facts of the case,
such as identifying the type of debt, the amount requested, or the specific income source
issue identified by the model 8 . This requires the mapping system to be intelligent,
capable of evaluating ranges and conditions within the kill event's data.
For instance, consider the LTV kill criterion. A static mapping might simply label all LTV-
related denials as "Collateral value is insufficient." However, a dynamic approach would
differentiate based on the actual loan-to-value percentage. A mapping matrix could be
configured as follows:
• If `LTV` > 90%, map to ECOA Reason Code 27: "The collateral value is
insufficient."
• If `LTV` is between 80% and 90%, map to ECOA Reason Code 26: "You requested
an amount that exceeds the maximum loan amount permitted by our regulations."
• If `LTV` is below 80% but the deal is killed due to a combination of other factors
(e.g., low FICO and high LTV), the system could still use Code 26 as the primary
reason, reflecting that the total financing package is the issue.
This principle of specificity extends to all kill criteria. For a low Debt Service Coverage
Ratio (DSCR), the system should analyze the underlying cause. If the low DSCR is
primarily driven by a high monthly payment (ADS), the reason could be "Your debt
payments or other obligations are too high." If it is driven by a low rent amount, the
reason should be "Not enough income." This level of detail requires the kill event to be
enriched with not just the final DSCR value, but also the calculated numerator (e.g.,
Rent) and denominator (e.g., PITIA).

mapped to ECOA/Reg B reason codes using this dynamic, evidence-based approach.

This nuanced mapping is only possible if the Explainability_Layer receives an
enriched kill event object. The object must contain not only the criterion that failed (e.g.,
"DSCR": "< 1.05") but also the full context: the calculated DSCR value, the

not only legally compliant but also genuinely informative, helping them understand the
precise factors that influenced the decision.
Navigating FCRA Disclosures for Third-Party Data
Sources
In addition to complying with ECOA's requirement for specific reasons for adverse action,
the Explainability_Layer must also adhere to the mandates of the Fair Credit
Reporting Act (FCRA). The FCRA imposes strict rules on the use of information from

===== PAGE 7 =====

Consumer Reporting Agencies (CRAs) in credit decisions 10. If a creditor uses a CRA's
information in taking an adverse action, the applicant must receive a specific disclosure
explaining this use 4 . This is a distinct requirement from the ECOA/Reg B reasons for
denial. The CFPB's enforcement actions against companies like 1st Alliance Lending
demonstrate the seriousness of this obligation; a key allegation was the company's failure
to provide the required adverse action notices when denying credit 10. Therefore, the
Adverse_Action_Notice_Payload generated by the Explainability_Layer
must contain two separate but related sections: the ECOA-mandated reasons for denial
and the FCRA-mandated disclosures regarding third-party data.
The first step is to track the use of third-party data throughout the underwriting process.
The engine may rely on CRAs for various pieces of information, such as rent payment
history, property valuations (Automated Valuation Models or AVMs), or credit scores.
When the engine evaluates a loan application, it must log which pieces of information
came from which CRA. If the final decision to deny the loan is based, even in part, on
information from a CRA, the FCRA requires a specific notification. The
Explainability_Layer must therefore inspect the decision path and, if a relevant
third-party data point was used, include the corresponding FCRA disclosure in the
payload. The standard FCRA disclosure statement typically reads something like: "We
obtained information about you from a consumer reporting agency. We are permitted by
law to provide this information to other persons. You have the right to request a free copy
of this report from the agency." The payload must also include the name and address of
the specific CRA that provided the information 10.
This dual-reporting structure adds complexity but is essential for compliance. The
Adverse_Action_Notice_Payload should be structured to clearly delineate these
two types of information. For example, it could have a top-level object containing the
standard ECOA notice text, followed by an array of reason_codes, and a separate top-
level field called fcra_disclosures containing an object with the CRA's name and the
required statutory text. This separation ensures that the ECOA reasons remain focused on
the applicant's financial standing, while the FCRA section fulfills the separate legal duty
to disclose the role of third parties. This practice is consistent with established
compliance frameworks, such as those governing asset-backed securities disclosure,
which require detailed and segregated reporting for different regulatory regimes 15.
While the initial implementation should focus on U.S. federal requirements, the long-
term strategy should acknowledge and plan for state-level variations. States like
California have enacted stricter privacy laws that may impose additional obligations 1 .
For example, California's regulations may require more detailed notices or provide

===== PAGE 8 =====

consumers with different rights regarding their data. The configurable nature of the
Explainability_Layer is key here. The configuration file for a lender operating in
California could include an additional block of state-specific language to be appended to
the payload, ensuring compliance across jurisdictions. This foresight will be crucial for
scaling the platform nationally without requiring a complete overhaul of the compliance
logic for each new state. By building a flexible architecture from the outset, the platform
can adapt to a complex and fragmented regulatory landscape, providing a seamless and
compliant experience for users and applicants nationwide.
Synthesis: The Technical Architecture and Payload
Specification for the Explainability_Layer
Based on the preceding analysis, the optimal technical architecture for the
Explainability_Layer involves a three-stage process: interception, enrichment, and
assembly. This process ensures that every automated loan denial is handled in a manner
that is both technically robust and legally defensible. First, the layer must intercept the
engine's output. Any event tagged as a KILL is routed to the Explainability_Layer
instead of being sent directly to the user interface or document generator. Second, the
layer must enrich the incoming kill event. The basic kill object, which currently contains
the failing criterion (e.g., "LTV > 80%"), must be augmented with a comprehensive set
of contextual data. This enriched object should include all raw inputs, all calculated
intermediate variables (e.g., loan_amount, appraised_value, purchase_price,
rent_monthly, pitia_monthly), and the specific numerical thresholds that were
breached. This data is the foundation for dynamic and specific reason code selection.
Third, the layer must assemble the final Adverse_Action_Notice_Payload. Using
the client's specific configuration and the enriched data, the layer selects the appropriate
ECOA/Reg B reason codes, generates the required specific textual explanations, and
incorporates any necessary FCRA disclosures for third-party data. The final output is a
structured, comprehensive payload ready for consumption.
The specification for the Adverse_Action_Notice_Payload is critical. It defines the
exact format and content required to satisfy regulatory obligations. The payload should
be a JSON object, making it easy to parse and integrate into various downstream systems.

Underwriting Engine can successfully fulfill its evolution from a powerful risk assessment
tool into a fully compliant, institutionally-viable platform for automated underwriting.
