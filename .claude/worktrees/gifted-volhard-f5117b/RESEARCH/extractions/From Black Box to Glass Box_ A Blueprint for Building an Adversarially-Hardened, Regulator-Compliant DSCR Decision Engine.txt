Phase I: Empirical Data Calibration and Matrix
Ingestion
The foundational mandate of the AEGIS DSCR engine is its commitment to being
calculation-first, deterministic, and grounded in empirical reality 37 38. This initial phase
of the research plan is dedicated to moving beyond theoretical formulas and mapping
them to the messy, dynamic reality of the commercial real estate and lending markets.
The objective is to replace abstract assumptions with verifiable data, creating a robust
and defensible foundation for every subsequent analytical layer. This involves three
critical streams of research: reverse-engineering the fragmented private market lender
matrix, calibrating stress shocks with geospatial precision, and establishing institutional
benchmarks for capital expenditures and liquidity reserves.
The first and most immediate challenge is to map the disparate world of non-QM and
DSCR lenders into a standardized, machine-readable format. Lenders such as CoreVest,
Visio, Kiavi, and Deephaven have unique underwriting guidelines that are not publicly
consolidated 123. This stream of work involves systematically ingesting and parsing rate
sheets, PDF guideline documents, and other public-facing materials from these
institutions. The primary task is to extract and structure their specific rules regarding the
intersection of FICO scores, Loan-to-Value (LTV) ratios, and Debt Service Coverage Ratio
(DSCR) thresholds 162. Beyond these basic metrics, the research must identify nuanced
restrictions, including property type classifications (e.g., rural properties, mixed-use
buildings, condo concentration limits), entity-vesting overlays, and any special
considerations for different borrower profiles 75146. The expected deliverable is a
version-controlled database, likely in JSON or YAML format, that represents this complex
web of lender rules. A crucial component of this deliverable is the implementation of
automated "staleness" decay functions; for instance, any lender matrix that has not been
updated within a defined period (e.g., 30 days) would automatically trigger a Gate 0:

Human Review, ensuring the engine operates on current, actionable information rather
than outdated guidelines . This process transforms a static set of rules into a living,
breathing component of the engine, directly addressing the weakness of standard
calculators that often rely on generic or obsolete lender criteria.
The second stream of calibration focuses on replacing arbitrary percentages in the
engine's stress-testing scenarios with empirically derived, geographically specific values.
The Tax Shock DSCR and Insurance Shock DSCR formulas are central to the
engine's ability to model post-purchase risk, but their effectiveness hinges entirely on
realistic shock parameters 37. The research plan directs a deep dive into the real-world
drivers of these shocks. For tax reassessment, this involves researching the significant
differences between states with disclosure regimes (like Texas, where property taxes are
assessed annually) and those with non-disclosure protections (like California under Prop
13) 55. By analyzing local millage rates and understanding the specific triggers for
reassessment, the team can build a Projected Tax API layer that dynamically adjusts
the tax shock assumption based on the property's exact coordinates. Similarly, the
volatility in commercial property insurance requires a sophisticated approach 13. The
research must integrate multiple data sources: FEMA flood maps to quantify flood risk,
wildfire risk scores from providers like First Street Foundation to assess fire peril, and
state-level FAIR plan data to understand the cost of last-resort coverage 68. By combining
these datasets, the engine can generate a ZIP-code-specific Insurance Shock DSCR
multiplier, reflecting the true risk profile of a given location. Industry data shows
premiums have spiked by over 20% in recent years, and insurance costs as a share of
income receivable have more than doubled since 2018, providing clear quantitative
anchors for these models 14 34. The final deliverable for this stream is a geospatial risk
lookup table that serves as a dynamic input for the Scenario & Stress-Test Engine,
ensuring that stress tests are not generic but tailored to the specific property being
analyzed.
The third and final stream of empirical research addresses the long-term health of the
asset itself by benchmarking capital expenditure (CapEx) and liquidity needs against
institutional standards. The CapEx Stress DSCR and Liquidity Survival Clock
are designed to move beyond simplistic OpEx calculations and force investors to account
for cyclical, major repairs 37. To make these metrics meaningful, they must be calibrated
against industry best practices. This research involves analyzing institutional operating
expense benchmarks from respected firms like John Burns and CBRE, as well as the
formal replacement reserve requirements mandated by government-sponsored
enterprises (GSEs) like Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac for multifamily assets 37116. The
output of this analysis will be an asset-class and property-age matrix. For example, the

Phase II: Mathematical Proof and Solver Validation
With the engine's formulas grounded in empirical data, the next critical phase is to
establish their mathematical rigor and computational stability. This phase moves from the
question of what to calculate to the question of how to calculate it correctly and reliably.
The core mandate is to ensure that the engine's innovative features—the Breakpoint
Solvers and the Fragility Indices—are not just theoretically sound but are also
numerically stable and computationally proven to be robust across a vast range of
conditions. This involves two distinct but related tasks: formally proving the convergence
of the algebraic and numerical solvers used for deal repair, and statistically validating the
survival and fragility indices against historical financial distress events. This work is
essential to prevent the engine from producing mathematically invalid or nonsensical
results, especially in extreme or edge-case scenarios.

===== PAGE 4 =====

The first task, the proof of solver convergence, targets the Deal-Repair Engine,
which is arguably the engine's most powerful feature for an advisor. Its purpose is to take
a failing deal and provide precise, actionable solutions, such as the Required Rent
Improvement or Required Price Reduction 37. These solutions are not simple
arithmetic; they require solving equations for variables that are embedded within other
formulas, such as DSCR. The research must begin with a formal algebraic proof that the
inversion of these formulas is valid. For example, the equation to solve for Required
Rent must be derived from the base DSCR formula,
NOI/AnnualDebtService≥DSCRtarget, and solved explicitly for the rent component,
isolating it on one side of the equation 81. This proves that a direct solution exists.
However, for more complex or stepped variables, such as the Break-even Refinance
Rate or the effect of changing the loan amount (which affects both debt service and
LTV), an algebraic solution may be intractable. In these cases, the engine must rely on
numerical methods like binary search or Newton-Raphson algorithms 37. The research
must validate that these iterative methods are implemented correctly and will always
converge to a solution if one exists, while gracefully handling cases where no solution is
possible (e.g., a deal that is structurally unsound). The deliverable for this task is a
comprehensive suite of unit tests—ideally running into the tens of thousands—that
subject every solver to a wide array of synthetic deals, including extreme edge cases like
zero interest rates, 100% vacancy, negative NOI, and near-zero cash flows. The goal is to
exhaustively test for solver convergence, divergence, and graceful degradation, providing
mathematical assurance that the Breakpoint & Repair Engine will never fail
catastrophically 151.
The second task involves the statistical calibration of the engine's forward-looking risk
indicators: the Composite Fragility Index (CFI) and the Investor Survival
Score (ISS) 37. While these scores are calculated from deterministic formulas, their
interpretation and thresholding depend on their demonstrated ability to predict real-
world outcomes. Therefore, this phase includes a backtesting exercise against historical
periods of severe financial stress. The research plan specifies looking at events like the
2008 Global Financial Crisis, the COVID-19 pandemic-induced market freeze, and the
sharp rate hikes of 2023, all of which caused widespread distress in the CRE and
mortgage sectors 37125. The process involves gathering historical data on CMBS distress
rates, SFR delinquency trends, and portfolio credit risk exposures during these periods 32
46. The engine's calculated CFI and ISS scores for a large sample of loans active during
those times would then be compared against the actual default and prepayment rates
observed 30. This allows the research team to create a statistical whitepaper that maps
the engine's scores to probabilities of default (PD) and loss given default (LGD),
effectively assigning real-world meaning to the 0-100 score ranges 89. For instance, the

===== PAGE 5 =====

analysis might reveal that deals with an ISS below 50 historically had a 35% probability
of negative cash flow in month 18, providing a concrete, evidence-based interpretation
for that score. Furthermore, this phase includes a rigorous review of the formulas
themselves for potential numerical instabilities. A prime example is the denominator in
the CFI formula, which must include a guard clause like max(Base Economic DSCR,
0.01) to prevent a catastrophic division-by-zero error when the economic DSCR is
hovering right at the 1.00x insolvency line 37. This attention to mathematical detail is
paramount for ensuring the engine's reliability and defensibility under scrutiny. The
successful completion of this phase provides the statistical backbone that validates the
engine's core diagnostic capabilities.
Phase III: Adversarial Red-Teaming and Model Risk
Governance
This phase operationalizes the directive to "act as a hostile model-risk validator," aligning
the project with established principles of robust AI governance, such as those outlined in
OCC Bulletin 2026-13 . The goal is not merely to test the engine under normal conditions
but to actively attempt to break it by injecting flawed, incomplete, or malicious data and
observing how the system responds. This adversarial red-teaming process rigorously tests
the engine's defenses, which are embodied in its confidence-scoring mechanisms, dual-
ledger separation logic, and architectural isolation of its core mathematics. The success of
this phase is measured by the engine's ability to successfully neutralize all attacks,
demonstrating its resilience and protecting the end-user from being misled by faulty
inputs or compromised logic.
The first major attack vector is Data Poisoning and Confidence Gate Testing. The
adversary's goal here is to feed the engine a deal with intentionally incorrect or
misleading data, such as inflated rent comps, stale expense figures, or missing critical
information like insurance costs. The defense mechanism is the Assumption
Confidence Score (ACS), which is designed to penalize deals built on weak data 37.
During this test, the red team will systematically inject various forms of poor-quality data.
For example, they might provide a rent figure with a source tag of "User" instead of a
verified lease agreement, or use a lender matrix that is known to be several months out of
date. The validation objective is twofold: first, to confirm that the ACS algorithm
correctly calculates a low confidence score (e.g., dropping below a predefined threshold
of 0.60); and second, to verify that this low score acts as a functional gate, halting the
recommendation engine and preventing a potentially dangerous recommendation from

===== PAGE 6 =====

being generated. The system should ideally present a bifurcated view, showing the "User-
Provided View" (with a clear warning about the low ACS) alongside an "Evidence-
Adjusted View" that might show a much bleaker outcome, empowering the user with
transparency rather than hiding the flaws . This test validates the engine's primary
defense against bad faith or uninformed users.
The second, and most critical, adversarial test is the "Qualifies-but-Dangerous" (QbD)
Stress Test. This scenario is designed to exploit the very weakness the engine was
created to solve: the disconnect between lender qualification and investor survival. The
red team will construct synthetic deals that are perfectly engineered to pass all the
lender's mathematical hurdles in Ledger 1. This could involve crafting a deal with a
DSCR that precisely meets the minimum threshold, a price point compliant with the
lender matrix, and seemingly reasonable expense assumptions. However, these deals will
contain hidden structural dangers that only surface in Ledger 2. Examples include a
massive deferred maintenance backlog that will require a large CapEx event shortly after
purchase, an impending payment shock from an interest-only (IO) loan that ends in year
five, or a property located in a neighborhood facing a significant, post-sale property tax
reassessment 37. The validation objective is to see if the QbD detector unequivocally
identifies these deals as dangerous. It must be shown that the engine accurately captures
the divergence between the passing DSCR_Lender and the failing DSCR_Economic (or
low ISS). Crucially, the QbD detector must be able to override a "Pass" verdict from the
Lender Qualification Engine and issue a strong warning, thus fulfilling its role as the
ultimate guardrail against mechanically approved but economically doomed investments.
This test is the ultimate validation of the dual-ledger architecture's efficacy.
The third attack vector is aimed at the integration of Artificial Intelligence, specifically
targeting the LLM-based narrative generation layer. The goal is to perform prompt-
injection attacks, attempting to trick the LLM into overriding the deterministic rules of
the engine, inventing a new lender overlay, or providing personalized financial advice
that falls outside the engine's strict advisory scope. The defense is architectural: the LLM
must be completely isolated from the engine's core logic. The research plan mandates
that the LLM's access is read-only, limited solely to the outputs of the Audit Log and
Formula Registry . It cannot modify any variables or bypass any decision gates. The
LLM's sole function is to act as a sophisticated natural language generator, populating a
library of locked narrative templates with the specific, deterministic variables produced
by the engine (e.g., "The primary failure driver is [Tax Shock], which reduces DSCR by
[0.18x]"). This prevents the LLM from becoming a black box that can be manipulated to
produce illicit or erroneous conclusions. The validation of this defense involves rigorous
security reviews of the API contracts and a thorough examination of the narrative
templates to ensure they are inherently safe and compliant, incapable of being twisted by

===== PAGE 7 =====

external prompts into providing regulated advice or circumventing safety checks 5 95.
Successfully defending against all three attack vectors demonstrates that the AEGIS DSCR
engine is not just a collection of smart formulas, but a hardened, robust, and trustworthy
financial decision-support infrastructure.
Phase IV: Regulatory Boundary Definition and Fair
Lending Research
Deploying a sophisticated decision-support tool in the financial sector carries significant
regulatory responsibilities. This phase is dedicated to navigating the complex legal
landscape to ensure the AEGIS DSCR engine operates safely and ethically. The research
focuses on three critical areas: defining the boundaries for adverse action notices under
the Equal Credit Opportunity Act (ECOA), establishing clear lines to avoid triggering the
Truth in Lending Act's Regulation Z (TRID), and proactively auditing the system for
potential biases to comply with fair lending principles. Failure to address these issues
could render even the most mathematically perfect engine legally unusable and expose
the organization to severe penalties. This work transforms the engine from a technical
artifact into a compliant product ready for professional deployment.
The first and most legally sensitive area is ECOA and Adverse Action Mapping, guided
by regulations like CFPB Circular 2022-03 91. If the engine is ever integrated into a
creditor's workflow, a denial of credit—even if initiated by the borrower using the tool—
must be accompanied by specific, accurate reasons for the adverse action. The engine's
abstract scores, such as the Dominant Risk Driver or a low ISS, cannot be the sole
explanation. The research must focus on creating a one-to-one mapping between the
engine's outputs and the standard ECOA adverse action reason codes 165. For example, a
denial based on a Stress DSCR falling below 1.0x due to a modeled tax shock must be
translatable into compliant, human-readable language that clearly explains the specific
risk. This requires close collaboration between quantitative researchers and legal experts
to translate the engine's internal logic into plain English statements that regulators will
accept. The deliverable is a comprehensive dictionary or mapping file that defines what
each engine score means in the context of a formal adverse action notice, ensuring that if
the engine says "No," it can also explain why in a legally defensible manner.
The second area of focus is defining the boundary between educational modeling and a
formal credit application under TRID. TRID imposes strict timelines and disclosure

===== PAGE 8 =====

requirements once a creditor receives a complete loan application 74. An improperly
triggered TRID process can lead to significant compliance failures. The research plan
mandates a careful definition of the API and UI boundaries that delineate when data
intake shifts from a user-driven scenario model to a formal application . For instance, the
system must have hard suppressions that prevent any output resembling a Loan Estimate
or a guaranteed rate lock unless the data flow is explicitly routed through a compliant
Loan Origination System (LOS) 112. The research must specify the exact point at which
the engine transitions from "advisor mode" to "creditor mode." This involves designing
clear user interfaces that distinguish between hypothetical questions ("What if my rent
goes down?") and mandatory application fields. The goal is to allow free-form
educational use while building an impenetrable wall around regulated activities,
preventing accidental violations of TRID's rules on good faith estimates and closing
disclosures 49.
The third pillar of regulatory research is Fair Lending and Bias Auditing. The engine's
reliance on geospatial data and statistical modeling creates a potential risk for digital
redlining, where proxies for protected classes inadvertently influence outcomes 41. For
example, using neighborhood-level vacancy or crime statistics as inputs could
disproportionately harm applicants from minority groups if those variables correlate with
race or ethnicity. The research plan calls for a proactive audit of all input variables to
identify and mitigate such proxy effects. This involves a deep dive into the data sources,
ensuring that the assumptions used in the engine are not rooted in discriminatory
practices. The research must explore whether newer technologies like Verifiable
Credentials and Decentralized Identifiers (VCs/DIDs) could help create more transparent
and auditable identity systems that reduce the potential for bias 120143. The deliverable is
a formal fairness impact assessment report, documenting the steps taken to audit the
model for bias and outlining any mitigation strategies. This proactive stance is crucial for
maintaining the trust of regulators and the public, and for ensuring the engine promotes
equitable access to credit rather than perpetuating historical inequities. This entire phase
is a critical step in translating the engine's powerful quantitative capabilities into a
responsible and legally compliant financial product.

Phase V: Cryptographic Audit Trail and Explainable AI
Architecture
This phase addresses two of the most critical aspects of deploying a high-stakes financial
decision engine: ensuring absolute trustworthiness through cryptographic immutability
and making complex, multi-faceted outputs comprehensible to human users. The engine's
design is fundamentally predicated on being a "glass box," not a "black box," where every
decision can be traced, reproduced, and understood 37 38. This requires a dual-pronged
approach. First, a state-of-the-art cryptographic audit trail must be built to provide
immutable proof of execution, satisfying demands from regulators, auditors, and internal
governance bodies. Second, a sophisticated Explainable AI (XAI) framework must be
developed to translate the engine's intricate Delta Ledger and probabilistic scores into
clear, actionable insights for investors, brokers, and underwriters. Together, these
components transform the engine from a mere calculator into a defensible and
trustworthy decision intelligence platform.
The cornerstone of the engine's trustworthiness is its Immutable Audit Trail. Every
calculation, input, and decision gate must be recorded in a log that is cryptographically
secure and tamper-evident 67193. The research plan specifies implementing SHA-256
hashing on the Audit Log Schema to achieve this . The audit record for any given
engine run must encapsulate a complete snapshot of the execution environment,
including the exact version of the engine software, the specific versions of the Formula
Registry and Lender Matrix Schema used, and the timestamp of the run 99. To
manage the potentially large volume of log entries efficiently, the plan points towards
using a Merkle tree data structure 36 70. A Merkle tree allows for efficient and secure
verification of the integrity of large datasets; one can prove that a specific input or
calculation was part of a particular engine run without needing to transmit or store the
entire log. For long-term security and to prepare for future threats like quantum
computing, the research should also consider incorporating post-quantum cryptographic
primitives into the design 65 72. The final deliverable is an exportable, machine-readable
JSON audit file. This file would serve as a permanent, verifiable record, allowing a
regulator or auditor to perfectly reconstruct the engine's state and logic at the exact
moment a decision was made, providing a level of defensibility and transparency that is
increasingly demanded in the financial industry 102210.
While the cryptographic trail ensures the engine's decisions are defensible, the XAI
architecture ensures they are understandable. The engine produces a wealth of
information, including the Delta Ledger which highlights the conflict between lender

===== PAGE 10 =====

and investor realities, the Breakpoint Map showing required fixes, and the Dominant
Risk Driver identifying the primary threat 37. Presenting this raw data to a user
would be overwhelming. The solution is a narrative template engineering process. The
research team must draft a comprehensive library of deterministic narrative blocks, each
corresponding to a specific recommendation class (e.g., "Strong Deal," "Financeable But
Fragile," "Qualifies But Dangerous") . These templates are "locked" and cannot be altered
by an LLM. Instead, the LLM's role is to act as a sophisticated string interpolator,
populating the templates with the specific, deterministic variables generated by the
engine. For example, a template for a "Fragile Deal" might contain the sentence: "This
deal is considered Financeable But Fragile because it fails under stress. The Dominant
Risk Driver is [Risk Factor], which is projected to reduce the DSCR by [Value]x. The
Liquidity Survival Clock indicates you would have approximately [Months] of cash
reserves remaining before breaching the minimum debt service requirement." The
research must conduct extensive UX testing to find the clearest and most effective way to
visualize the Delta Ledger conflict, perhaps using color-coding, split-screen views, or
interactive charts that allow the user to toggle between the Lender View and the
Economic Reality View 137. The final deliverable is a complete, tested UI/UX
specification for the 11-part user report, ensuring that the engine's profound analytical
power is translated into intuitive, actionable intelligence for the end-user.
Final Synthesis: The Integrated AEGIS DSCR Engine and
Implementation Roadmap
The culmination of this research plan is the synthesis of a single, cohesive, and
production-grade Advisor-grade DSCR Decision Engine. This is not a monolithic
application but a carefully orchestrated nine-module pipeline, where data flows strictly
downward from intake to recommendation, with the Audit & Compliance Layer
acting as an immutable ledger and a final checkpoint at every stage 37. The entire system
is built on the four pillars of Calculation-First logic, a Dual-Ledger architecture,
Adversarial Robustness, and Strict Compliance Boundaries. This final section outlines the
integrated architecture and provides a phased, five-phase roadmap for its development
and validation, ensuring a disciplined and methodical path from concept to a deployable,
institution-grade financial infrastructure.
The engine's architecture is a sequential pipeline of specialized modules: 1. Data Intake
& Confidence Layer: The entry point where all user inputs are tagged with metadata

===== PAGE 11 =====

detailing their source (e.g., Verified Lease, User-Guessed, Stale API) and timestamp. This
feeds the Assumption Confidence Score (ACS) algorithm. 2. Deterministic
Formula Engine: The mathematical core that executes all baseline calculations (NOI,
PITIA, DSCR, etc.) and the initial Baseline Metrics. 3. Lender Qualification
Engine: Applies the versioned lender matrix rules to the baseline metrics, acting as the
first pass/fail gate. 4. Investor Survival Engine: Runs a 36-month monthly cash flow
simulation based on the deal's specifics, calculating the Liquidity Survival Clock
(LSC) and the Investor Survival Score (ISS). 5. Scenario & Stress Engine:
Automatically generates and evaluates Base, Conservative, and Severe stress scenarios,
recalculating metrics like Tax Shock DSCR and Vacancy Shock DSCR for each. 6.
Breakpoint & Repair Engine: If the deal fails under certain conditions, this module
activates to calculate the exact dollar or percentage changes needed to fix the deal
(Δ_Rent, Δ_Price, Δ_LTV) and assesses the realism of these fixes with the Deal
Repairability Score (DRS). 7. Risk Diagnosis Engine: Ranks the identified risks
using the DSCR Fragility Score (DFS) and other diagnostics to determine the
Dominant Risk Driver. 8. Recommendation Engine: The synthesis layer. It takes
the outputs from all preceding modules—including the Delta Ledger's conflict signal
—and applies the gated logic to generate the final, human-readable recommendation. 9.
Audit & Compliance Layer: The unifying element. It logs every single variable,
calculation, and decision gate from all modules into a cryptographically secured,
immutable log before the final recommendation is delivered.
To manage this complexity and ensure quality, the engineering teams will follow a
mandated five-phase Minimum Viable Product (MVP) build sequence, with a clear gate to
proceed to the next phase.
