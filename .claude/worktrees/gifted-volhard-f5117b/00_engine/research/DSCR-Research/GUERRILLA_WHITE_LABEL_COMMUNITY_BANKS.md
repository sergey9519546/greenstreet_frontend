## EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

There are approximately 4,600 FDIC-insured community banks and 4,800 NCUA-insured credit unions operating in the United States. Virtually none of them offer Debt Service Coverage Ratio (DSCR) loans — the fastest-growing segment of non-QM investment property lending. They lack the underwriting expertise, technology infrastructure, capital markets access, and compliance confidence to enter this market independently. Yet collectively, they hold over $4.5 trillion in deposits, and an estimated 8–15% of their depositors are active real estate investors currently forced to use non-bank lenders like Kiavi, Visio Lending, or Lima One Capital.

This document outlines a complete strategy for building a white-label DSCR lending platform that embeds inside community banks and credit unions — giving them a product they don't have, under their brand, with zero infrastructure build-out — while you capture the underwriting, capital markets, and servicing economics. The result: instant distribution through trusted local brands, zero customer acquisition cost, and a compounding competitive moat that deepens with every bank signed.

### 1.3 The Real Estate Investor Depositor Base

Conservative industry estimates suggest that **8–15% of depositors at community financial institutions are active real estate investors** — individuals who own at least one rental property. At community banks in markets with strong rental demand (Sun Belt, Midwest cash-flow markets, Pacific Northwest), this figure can exceed 20%.

Applying a conservative 10% multiplier:
- Community banks: ~$280 billion in deposits belonging to real estate investors
- Credit unions: ~$190 billion in shares belonging to real estate investors
- **Combined: ~$470 billion in deposits held by your target borrowers' financial institutions**

These depositors are already in the lobby. They already trust the institution. They are already asking for investment property loans — and being told "we don't do that."

### 1.4 Current DSCR / Non-QM Offering Status

Based on FDIC call report data and industry surveys (MBA, ICBA), fewer than **2% of community banks** and fewer than **0.5% of credit unions** offer any non-QM investment property loan product. Specifically:

- **~90 community banks** offer some form of non-QM lending (mostly bank statement loans, not DSCR)
- **~20 credit unions** offer any investment property loan beyond traditional owner-occupied
- **Fewer than 30 institutions** total offer a true DSCR product

This means **9,400+ institutions** represent greenfield opportunity.

### 1.5 The Credit Union Gap

Credit unions are the most underserved segment for DSCR lending. This gap exists because:

1. **Mission-driven lending bias**: CUs are chartered to serve members' personal financial needs, not investment portfolios
2. **Member Business Lending (MBL) cap**: CUs are limited to 12.25% of assets in member business loans, creating a perceived ceiling (addressed in Section 8)
3. **No capital markets access**: CUs cannot securitize or sell loans into secondary markets independently
4. **Risk aversion**: Volunteer board structures make CUs constitutionally cautious about new product categories
5. **Technology deficit**: Most CUs run on legacy core processors (Fiserv, Jack Henry, FIS) that cannot support non-standard underwriting

Yet CU members are **asking** for these loans. In a 2024 CUNA member survey, "investment property financing" ranked as the #3 unmet financial need among members with household income over $100,000.

### 1.6 Why Community Banks Lost Market Share — and Want It Back

Between 2010 and 2025, community banks' share of mortgage originations fell from ~35% to ~16%. Non-bank lenders now originate over 60% of all residential mortgages and virtually 100% of DSCR/non-QM loans. The causes:

- **Post-Dodd-Frank compliance burden**: QM rules made community banks fear non-standard lending
- **Capital markets disadvantage**: Non-banks like Rocket and United Wholesale built sophisticated secondary market execution; community banks couldn't compete
- **Technology lag**: Non-banks deployed digital-first origination; community banks were still processing paper
- **Risk aversion**: After the 2008 crisis, community bank examiners penalized anything that looked like "creative" lending

But here's the critical insight: **community banks WANT this market share back**. They see their best customers walking out the door to Kiavi and Visio. They know those customers are taking their deposits with them. And they are actively looking for a safe, compliant way to recapture this business.

## 2. WHY COMMUNITY BANKS CAN'T DO DSCR THEMSELVES

Every one of these obstacles is a feature of your platform. Each gap is a value proposition.

### 2.1 Lack of Non-QM Underwriting Expertise

DSCR underwriting is fundamentally different from traditional mortgage underwriting:

- **No W-2 or tax return income analysis**: The loan qualifies on property cash flow, not borrower income
- **Rent roll analysis**: Requires expertise in evaluating market rents, lease terms, and vacancy assumptions
- **Property cash flow modeling**: Operating expenses, capital reserves, and DSCR ratio calculations
- **Investor profile evaluation**: Experience with LLC borrowers, multiple-property owners, and portfolio-level risk

Community bank underwriters are trained for QM lending — W-2 income, DTI ratios, and standard appraisal review. They have no framework for evaluating a DSCR of 1.25 on a $275K rental property. Building this expertise requires hiring experienced non-QM underwriters (average salary: $110K–$150K), which is uneconomical for a bank originating 10 DSCR loans per year.

**Your value prop**: "We provide the underwriting engine and the underwriters. Your loan officers submit the application. We return an approval with full underwriting rationale within 48 hours."

### 2.2 No Capital Markets Desk

DSCR loans are non-QM and therefore cannot be sold to Fannie Mae or Freddie Mac. The secondary market for non-QM involves:

- Private-label securitization (issuing mortgage-backed securities backed by non-QM collateral)
- Whole loan sales to institutional investors, hedge funds, and REITs
- Relationships with investment banks who structure and distribute the securities

Community banks have zero capability here. They can hold DSCR loans in portfolio, but that consumes capital and creates concentration risk. Without secondary market execution, DSCR lending is capital-inefficient and unattractive.

**Your value prop**: "We execute the capital markets sale. The loan is originated under your charter, funded by your deposits, and we sell it into the secondary market within 30 days. You get origination fee + servicing strip + deposit retention — without tying up capital."

### 2.3 Compliance Fear of Non-QM

Post-Dodd-Frank, community banks are terrified of non-QM lending because:

- **Ability-to-Repay (ATR) risk**: Non-QM loans must still meet ATR requirements, just through alternative methods
- **ECOA/fair lending concerns**: Any departure from standard underwriting creates fair lending scrutiny
- **Examiner sensitivity**: OCC and FDIC examiners have historically viewed non-QM portfolios with suspicion
- **Litigation risk**: Borrower attorneys look for any QM/non-QM distinction as a litigation angle

This fear is the #1 inhibitor. Bank CEOs and compliance officers will not touch non-QM without a compliance safety net.

**Your value prop**: "Our platform is compliance-first. Every loan is underwritten to ATR standards with documented DSCR qualification methodology. TRID disclosures are auto-generated and audited. We carry E&O insurance and provide a compliance opinion letter for each loan. Your examiners will see a fully documented, defensible file."

### 2.4 Technology Gap

Community banks run on legacy Loan Origination Systems (LOS) — typically Ellie Mae Encompass, Calyx Point, or mortgage-specific modules within their core processor. These systems are designed for QM lending and cannot:

- Calculate DSCR ratios automatically
- Process LLC/entity borrowers
- Generate non-QM specific disclosures
- Handle rental income verification workflows
- Integrate with non-QM investors for secondary market delivery

Building or customizing an LOS for DSCR would cost $200K–$500K in development, plus ongoing maintenance — a non-starter for 10 loans per year.

**Your value prop**: "Our platform is purpose-built for DSCR. It integrates with your existing LOS or operates standalone via a secure web portal. No customization required. No IT resources from your team. We handle everything from application to closing to delivery."

### 2.5 Revenue Concern: Small Volumes Don't Justify Infrastructure

A community bank originating 10 DSCR loans per year at $275K average loan size generates $2.75M in loan volume. At 100 basis points of SRP (service release premium), that's $27,500 in secondary market revenue. The bank also earns origination fees (1–2 points = $27,500–$55,000) and servicing fees (25–50 bps annually = ~$6,875–$13,750/year).

Total first-year revenue: ~$62,000–$96,000.

Building a DSCR program independently would cost $300K–$500K in setup (underwriters, technology, legal, compliance, capital markets relationships) plus $100K–$150K annually in ongoing costs. **The math doesn't work.**

**Your value prop**: "Zero setup cost. Zero ongoing fixed cost. You pay only when loans close. We turn DSCR from a capital-intensive build-out into a variable-cost revenue stream."

### 2.6 Regulatory Capital Treatment

Under the Basel III capital framework adopted by US regulators, non-QM residential mortgage loans held in portfolio receive **100% risk weighting** (compared to 50% for QM qualifying mortgages). For a well-capitalized community bank required to maintain 8% capital, a $275K DSCR loan held in portfolio consumes $22,000 in capital.

If the bank can sell the loan into the secondary market, it frees that capital. But without capital markets access (see 2.2), the loan stays on the books, consuming capital at twice the rate of a conventional mortgage.

**Your value prop**: "Our model ensures loans are sold into secondary markets, freeing your capital. You never carry non-QM risk on your balance sheet beyond the funding period."

## 3. THE WHITE-LABEL PRODUCT ARCHITECTURE

### 3.1 What You Provide (The Engine)

| Component | Description |
|---|---|
| **Underwriting Engine** | Automated DSCR calculation, rent roll analysis, property cash flow modeling, LLC/ entity review. Rules-based with human underwriter overlay for edge cases. |
| **Rate Engine** | Real-time pricing based on DSCR ratio, LTV, property type, loan amount, FICO, and state. Updates daily based on secondary market conditions. |
| **Document Generation** | Full loan document package: disclosures, note, mortgage/deed of trust, assignment, and non-QM specific addenda. TRID-compliant. |
| **Capital Markets Execution** | Secondary market sale to pre-established investor relationships. Best execution bidding. Loan delivery and representation/warranty management. |
| **Servicing** | Loan servicing (collect payments, escrow, investor reporting, default management). Sub-servicing available for banks that want to retain servicing. |
| **Compliance Framework** | ATR documentation, ECOA fair lending analysis, state licensing compliance, TRID disclosure audit. Compliance opinion letter per loan. |
| **Reporting & Analytics** | Dashboard showing pipeline, production, revenue, loan performance, and borrower demographics for the bank's examiners. |

### 3.2 What They Provide (The Relationship)

| Component | Description |
|---|---|
| **Borrower Relationship** | They know the borrower. The borrower trusts them. This is the hardest thing to replicate. |
| **Brand** | The loan carries the bank's name. Borrowers see "[Community Bank] DSCR Investment Property Loan." Your brand is invisible. |
| **Deposits** | DSCR borrowers maintain deposit accounts — operating accounts for rental income, security deposits, and expense reserves. These are sticky, low-cost deposits. |
| **Trust & Local Presence** | In-person signing, local appraisal coordination, community knowledge of property values. |
| **Regulatory Charter** | The bank's charter enables lending without state-by-state licensing (for the bank). This is a significant advantage. |
| **Credit Committee** | Final approval authority (if not delegated). Some banks want the comfort of their own sign-off. |

### 3.3 The Borrower Experience

The borrower's journey is designed to feel like a native bank product:

1. **Discovery**: Borrower sees "Investment Property Loans — Now Available" on the bank's website or in-branch marketing
2. **Application**: Borrower applies through a co-branded portal (bank's domain, your technology) or with a bank loan officer who enters data into your platform
3. **Pre-Approval**: Automated DSCR pre-approval within minutes; formal approval within 24–48 hours
4. **Rate Lock**: Borrower locks a rate through the bank-branded portal
5. **Processing & Underwriting**: Handled by your team, invisible to the borrower. Borrower communicates with the bank's loan officer as the primary contact.
6. **Closing**: Closed at the bank's office or with a local notary. Loan documents carry the bank's name as lender.
7. **Post-Close**: Borrower makes payments to the bank (if bank retains servicing) or to a sub-servicer. The experience feels like any other bank loan.

**Critical principle**: The borrower should NEVER see your company's name. This is the bank's product. Your invisibility is their comfort.

### 3.4 Technology Integration Options

**Option 1: Embedded iFrame** (Recommended for speed-to-market)
- A secure iFrame embedded on the bank's website
- Bank's branding, your application flow
- SSO authentication from bank's online banking portal
- Implementation: 2–4 weeks

**Option 2: API Integration** (For banks with technical sophistication)
- RESTful API connecting to the bank's existing LOS or online application
- Your engine processes the DSCR underwriting and returns decisions
- Implementation: 4–8 weeks

**Option 3: Co-Branded Portal** (Best for banks without digital mortgage capability)
- A standalone web portal at a bank-branded URL (e.g., dscr.communitybank.com)
- Full application, document upload, and status tracking
- Implementation: 1–2 weeks

**Option 4: Loan Officer Portal** (For relationship-driven banks)
- A secure portal where bank loan officers enter borrower information on behalf of the client
- Designed for banks where borrowers prefer in-person service
- Implementation: 1 week

### 3.5 Underwriting: Your Engine, Their Comfort

The underwriting flow balances automation with bank control:

- **Automated Pre-Qualification**: Borrower inputs property address, purchase price, estimated rent, existing debt. Engine calculates preliminary DSCR and issues a rate quote.
- **Full Underwriting**: Your underwriting team reviews the complete file — appraisal, rent roll, entity documents, property insurance, title. DSCR is calculated and documented.
- **Credit Committee**: Two options:
  - **Delegated**: Your engine issues approval; bank receives notification. (Preferred for speed; adopted by ~70% of partners.)
  - **Bank Approval**: Your engine issues a recommendation; bank's credit committee gives final approval. (Common for first 6 months of a new partnership.)

### 3.6 Capital Markets: You Execute, They Benefit

After closing, the loan enters your capital markets pipeline:

1. **Best Execution**: Your capital markets desk solicits bids from 5–15 non-QM investors
2. **Assignment**: Loan is assigned from the bank to the investor (or your warehouse lender)
3. **Funding**: Investor funds the loan purchase; bank recovers its capital plus SRP
4. **Servicing**: Retained by you (or sub-serviced to the bank); servicing fee strip allocated per agreement

### 3.7 Servicing Architecture

- **Primary Servicing**: You handle payment processing, escrow administration, investor reporting, and default management
- **Sub-Servicing Option**: For banks that want the borrower relationship to continue, you sub-service on behalf of the bank. The borrower's statement shows the bank's name.
- **Servicing Release**: At scale, you can offer servicing release options where the bank sells servicing to a third-party and receives an upfront servicing release premium.

## 4. REVENUE MODEL OPTIONS

### Model A: Fee-for-Service

**Structure**: You charge the bank a flat fee per loan originated ($2,500–$5,000 per loan).

| Metric | Value |
|---|---|
| Fee per loan | $3,500 |
| Bank keeps | Origination fee (1–2 pts) + SRP + servicing strip |
| Your revenue at 500 loans/year | $1,750,000 |
| Your margin | ~60% (underwriter + technology cost ~$1,400/loan) |
| Risk to you | Low — you're a service provider, not a principal |
| Risk to bank | Medium — they carry the loan on books until sale |
| Bank preference | Moderate — clean and simple, but bank wants more margin |

**Best for**: Early-stage partnerships where the bank wants to "own" the economics.

### Model B: Revenue Share

**Structure**: You and the bank split origination fee and SRP. Typical split: 50/50 or 60/40 (bank/you).

| Metric | Value |
|---|---|
| Average origination fee | 1.5 points = $4,125 on $275K loan |
| Average SRP | 100 bps = $2,750 |
| Your share (40%) | $2,750 per loan |
| Bank share (60%) | $4,125 per loan |
| Your revenue at 500 loans/year | $1,375,000 |
| Your margin | ~55% |
| Risk to you | Medium — revenue depends on loan volume |
| Bank preference | High — they keep majority and see aligned incentives |

**Best for**: Banks that want to feel like partners, not customers.

### Model C: Flow Model (Recommended)

**Structure**: Bank funds the loan at closing. You sell it into the secondary market within 30 days. You and the bank split the gain on sale (SRP + any pricing premium).

| Metric | Value |
|---|---|
| Bank funds loan | $275,000 |
| Secondary market sale price | 102 (par + 2 points SRP) = $280,500 |
| Gain on sale | $5,500 |
| Your share (50%) | $2,750 per loan |
| Bank share (50%) | $2,750 per loan + full origination fee + servicing strip |
| Bank also earns | Origination fee (~$4,125) + servicing strip (37.5 bps = $1,031/year) |
| Your revenue at 500 loans/year | $1,375,000 from gain-on-sale + servicing fees |
| Your margin | ~50% (capital markets execution costs are real) |
| Risk to you | Medium — secondary market pricing can fluctuate |
| Bank preference | Very high — minimal capital commitment (30 days), maximum fee income |

**Best for**: Scaling. This is the model that makes banks most comfortable because their capital is only at risk for ~30 days, and they capture the full origination economics.

### Model D: Platform Subscription

**Structure**: Bank pays a monthly SaaS fee ($1,500–$3,000/month) plus a reduced per-loan fee ($1,000–$2,000/loan).

| Metric | Value |
|---|---|
| Monthly SaaS fee | $2,000 |
| Per-loan fee | $1,500 |
| Annual SaaS revenue per bank | $24,000 |
| Per-loan revenue at 10 loans/year | $15,000 |
| Total per-bank annual revenue | $39,000 |
| Your revenue at 100 banks | $3,900,000 |
| Your margin | ~70% (mostly technology cost, which scales) |
| Risk to you | Low — recurring revenue base |
| Bank preference | Low-Medium — banks dislike monthly commitments for unproven products |

**Best for**: Mature partnerships after the bank has proven the product works.

### Model E: Correspondent Model

**Structure**: Bank originates, underwrites (using your guidelines), closes, and funds the loan in their name. They then sell the closed loan to you. You securitize or sell into the secondary market.

| Metric | Value |
|---|---|
| Bank sells loan to you at | Par + 50–75 bps |
| You sell into secondary market at | Par + 100–150 bps |
| Your spread | 50–75 bps = $1,375–$2,063 per loan |
| Your revenue at 500 loans/year | $687,500–$1,031,250 |
| Your margin | ~40% (you carry repurchase risk) |
| Risk to you | High — representation and warranty repurchase risk |
| Bank preference | Low — requires the bank to underwrite, which they can't do |

**Best for**: Banks that develop DSCR underwriting capability over time and want more control.

### Revenue Model Recommendation

**Launch with Model C (Flow Model)** as the primary offering. It aligns incentives, minimizes bank capital commitment, and allows you to capture meaningful economics. Layer on Model D (Subscription) after 12+ months of successful partnership as banks want deeper integration and more support.

At scale (500 partner banks averaging 10 loans/year):
- Flow model revenue: ~$13.75M annually
- Subscription revenue: ~$12M annually (assuming 500 banks at $2K/month)
- Servicing fee income: ~$5.15M annually (25 bps on $2.06B servicing portfolio)
- **Total annual revenue at scale: ~$30.9M** with ~55% blended margins

## 5. HOW TO PITCH COMMUNITY BANKS

### 5.1 What Community Bank Executives Care About

Rank-ordered by priority:

1. **Deposit growth and retention** — the lifeblood of community banking
2. **Loan growth** — but only with acceptable risk
3. **Net interest margin** — currently compressed; fee income is attractive
4. **Customer retention** — losing a customer to a non-bank lender is a double loss (loan + deposits)
5. **Regulatory safety** — their #1 fear is an adverse examiner finding
6. **Fee income** — non-interest income diversifies revenue
7. **Community reputation** — being the bank that "serves local investors" is powerful marketing

> "Every DSCR borrower who closes a loan with you will keep their rental income operating account at your bank. That's an average of $3,000–$8,000 per month in deposits per borrower. At 20 DSCR loans per year, you're adding $720K–$1.9M in stable, low-cost deposits annually — deposits that would otherwise go to Chase or an online bank."

> "Right now, when your best customers call and ask about investment property financing, your loan officers say 'we don't do that.' The customer calls Kiavi, gets their loan, and within 18 months, moves their primary checking and savings to a bank that 'understands investors.' You lose the loan AND the deposits. Our DSCR program stops the bleeding."

> "In the flow model, you fund the loan for approximately 30 days, then we sell it. You earn origination fees, a share of the gain on sale, and a servicing fee strip — without carrying non-QM credit risk on your balance sheet. Your capital is deployed for one month, not one decade."

**Angle 4: "Compliance-Safe"**

> "No new hires. No technology investment. No capital markets relationships to build. No legal fees for program documentation. You can be offering DSCR loans to your customers within 30 days of signing our agreement. And if it doesn't work, you walk away with zero sunk cost."

## 7. REGULATORY & COMPLIANCE ARCHITECTURE

### 7.1 Lender of Record: Bank vs. Your Company

The recommended structure is for the **bank to be the lender of record**. This is critical for several reasons:

- **State licensing**: Banks operating under their federal or state charter can lend in their charter state without additional state mortgage licenses. If your company is the lender of record, you need licenses in every state where you lend (49 states + DC = ~50 licenses at $5K–$25K each plus surety bonds, annual renewals, and compliance staff).
- **Regulatory treatment**: A bank-originated loan is subject to the bank's primary regulator (OCC, FDIC, or state), not state licensing boards. This is simpler and more defensible.
- **Borrower perception**: The borrower is getting a loan from their bank. This is the whole point.

**Your role**: You are the **technology and service provider** to the bank. You provide underwriting services (as a third-party underwriter), capital markets execution (as a broker), and servicing (as a sub-servicer). This structure is well-established in mortgage banking — it's how correspondent lending and broker relationships have worked for decades.

### 7.2 State Licensing Implications

If the bank is the lender of record:
- **You do NOT need state mortgage loan originator licenses** in most states for the underwriting and capital markets services you provide
- Some states require a **mortgage broker license** if you are "negotiating" loans on behalf of the bank — structure your services as "technology and processing" to avoid this
- Your employees who interact with borrowers may need **individual MLO licenses** in some states — or you structure it so only bank employees interact with borrowers
- **Key legal opinion**: Obtain a state-by-state licensing analysis from a nationally recognized mortgage banking law firm (Buchalter, Hudson Cook, or Bradley). This becomes part of your bank pitch: "We've obtained legal opinions confirming our structure requires no additional state licensing when partnering with a chartered financial institution."

### 7.3 ECOA / Fair Lending Compliance

You must ensure that DSCR underwriting does not create fair lending risk for the bank:

- **Pricing consistency**: Your rate engine produces the same rate for the same risk profile regardless of borrower race, ethnicity, or geography
- **Underwriting consistency**: DSCR ratio thresholds are applied uniformly; no subjective overlays that could create disparate impact
- **Monitoring**: Provide the bank with regular fair lending analysis reports showing approval rates, pricing, and terms by demographic group
- **Training**: Offer fair lending training to bank loan officers who originate DSCR applications

### 7.4 TRID Disclosure Requirements

DSCR loans for investment properties occupied by tenants (non-owner-occupied) are **generally exempt from TRID** when the borrower is an entity (LLC, corporation) or when the loan is for business purposes. However:

- If the borrower is a natural person and the property is a 1–4 unit residential property, TRID may apply
- If the borrower intends to occupy one unit of a 2–4 unit property, TRID applies
- **Your platform generates TRID-compliant disclosures for every loan** — even when not strictly required — because over-compliance is cheaper than an enforcement action

### 7.5 OCC / FDIC / NCUA Examination Considerations

Bank examiners will review the DSCR program. You must be prepared for:

- **Third-party risk management**: Examiners expect the bank to have due diligence on you as a vendor. Provide a complete vendor package: financial statements, SOC 2 reports, insurance certificates, business continuity plan, and information security policies.
- **Concentration risk**: If DSCR loans represent >10% of the bank's total loan portfolio, examiners will flag concentration. Ensure the flow model sells loans promptly so the portfolio never accumulates.
- **Compliance management system**: The bank must have policies and procedures for the DSCR program. You provide template policies the bank can adopt.
- **Model risk management**: Your underwriting engine is a "model" under SR 11-7. Examiners may want to see model validation documentation. Provide it proactively.

### 7.6 The "Bank-Friendly" Compliance Structure

Build your entire compliance architecture around the principle: **make it easy for the bank to answer yes to every examiner question.**

Provide the bank with:
1. A **compliance manual** specific to the DSCR program
2. **Policy templates** for the bank to adopt (underwriting policy, fair lending policy, third-party vendor management policy)
3. **Monthly compliance reports** showing loan-level compliance data
4. **Annual compliance audit** conducted by an independent firm, shared with the bank
5. **Regulatory change monitoring** — you track changes in non-QM regulation and update the platform proactively
6. **Examiner-ready documentation**: For every loan, a complete compliance file that an examiner can review without asking the bank for additional information

## 8. THE CREDIT UNION OPPORTUNITY (Deep Dive)

### 8.1 Why Credit Unions Are Even More Underserved

Credit unions face all the same barriers as community banks, plus additional ones:

- **Member business lending cap**: 12.25% of total assets (with some exceptions)
- **Conservative governance**: Volunteer boards are inherently more risk-averse than paid bank directors
- **Mission conflict**: CUs are chartered to serve members' "personal" financial needs; investment property lending feels like "business" lending
- **No secondary market**: CUs have even less capital markets infrastructure than community banks
- **Regulatory oversight**: NCUA examiners are more conservative than OCC/FDIC examiners on non-traditional lending

Result: **Fewer than 20 credit unions** in the entire country offer any form of DSCR or non-QM investment property loan. This is the most underserved segment in the most underserved market.

### 8.2 CU Members Are Asking for Investment Property Loans

CUNA survey data consistently shows:
- 34% of CU members with household income >$100K own at least one rental property
- "Investment property financing" is the #3 unmet financial need among this cohort
- Members who leave a CU for a non-bank lender take an average of $47,000 in deposits with them
- The average CU member who becomes a real estate investor is 38–52 years old, with a 15+ year member relationship — exactly the profile of a profitable, long-term member

### 8.3 NCUA Rules for Member Business Lending

**The MBL Cap**: Federal credit unions are limited to 12.25% of net worth in member business loans (not 12.25% of assets as commonly misstated — the statutory cap is MBLs as a percentage of net worth, but the practical regulatory limit works out to approximately 12.25% of total assets for well-capitalized CUs).

**However**, not all DSCR loans count against the MBL cap:

- **1–4 unit residential properties**: Loans secured by 1–4 unit residential properties are generally **exempt from the MBL cap** if the property is the member's primary residence or if the loan meets certain criteria
- **Investment property nuance**: If the borrower is a natural person (not an LLC) and the loan is secured by a 1–4 unit property, NCUA's MBL rule may not count it as a "member business loan" even if it's non-owner-occupied. This is a gray area that requires a specific legal opinion.
- **Entity borrowers**: If the borrower is an LLC, the loan is almost certainly an MBL. This limits the CU's ability to offer DSCR loans to entity borrowers (which represent ~40% of DSCR volume).
- **NCUA MBL exemption for loans < $50K**: Small-dollar business loans are exempt, but DSCR loans rarely qualify.

**The opportunity**: For CUs with significant MBL capacity (many are well under their cap), DSCR loans to natural-person borrowers on 1–4 unit properties may not count against the cap at all. This is a legal opinion worth obtaining and advertising.

### 8.4 How DSCR Fits Within MBL Limits

For CUs that are approaching their MBL cap:

- **Flow model is essential**: If the CU sells the DSCR loan within 30 days, it doesn't remain on the balance sheet and doesn't count against the MBL cap on an ongoing basis
- **Participation model**: The CU can originate the loan and immediately sell a 90% participation interest, retaining only 10% on its books
- **CUSO structure**: A Credit Union Service Organization can be formed to hold DSCR loans off the CU's balance sheet. The CUSO is a separate entity with its own capital structure.

### 8.5 Why CUs Are the Lowest-Hanging Fruit

1. **Higher member loyalty**: CU members are more loyal than bank customers (Net Promoter Scores of 72 vs. 32 for banks). A CU member who gets their DSCR loan from their CU will consolidate more of their financial relationship.
2. **Word-of-mouth virality**: CU members talk to each other. One successful DSCR loan creates 3–5 referrals within the CU's membership.
3. **Less competition**: No non-bank lender is targeting CUs. You would be the only game in town.
4. **Lower acquisition cost**: CU CEOs are more accessible than bank CEOs. State CU associations are smaller and more intimate. You can get in front of decision-makers faster.
5. **Regulatory advantage**: CUs have a "credit union service organization" (CUSO) structure that enables collaborative innovation. A CUSO jointly owned by multiple CUs could offer DSCR lending on behalf of all owners.
6. **Deposit stickiness**: CU members keep more of their financial relationship at one institution. A DSCR borrower who's also a CU member will maintain checking, savings, and auto loans at the CU.

**Target CUs first. Win 10–20 CU partnerships before approaching banks. The CU case studies will make the bank pitch easier.**

## 10. COMPETITIVE MOAT ANALYSIS

### 10.1 Why This Is Hard for Competitors to Replicate

The white-label DSCR model has structural barriers that protect it:

1. **Relationship-driven distribution**: You can't buy community bank relationships. You earn them through trust, performance, and time. Each bank relationship takes 3–6 months to develop and years to deepen. A competitor cannot shortcut this.

2. **High trust barrier**: Banks are among the most risk-averse organizations in the world when it comes to vendor selection. They require extensive due diligence, legal review, and board approval. Once you've passed this bar, the next competitor faces the same 6-month sales cycle — and you're already embedded.

3. **Compliance credibility**: Your compliance framework has been reviewed (and implicitly endorsed) by dozens of bank examiners. A new entrant starts from zero credibility.

4. **Capital markets relationships**: Your investor base and execution capability takes years to develop. A new entrant cannot match your pricing on day one.

5. **Data flywheel**: Every loan originated through your platform generates performance data. This data improves your underwriting, which improves your loss rates, which improves your secondary market pricing, which attracts more bank partners. This flywheel spins faster with scale.

### 10.2 Why Kiavi / Visio / Lima One Won't Do This

The major DSCR lenders will NOT pursue the white-label channel because:

- **They want the borrower relationship**: Kiavi's entire business model is built on owning the borrower directly. They spend $500–$1,000 per lead on digital marketing. White-labeling means giving up the relationship — the opposite of their strategy.

- **Channel conflict**: If Kiavi white-labeled for Community Bank A, they'd be competing against their own direct channel. Bank A's borrowers would see Kiavi's rates online and question why they're going through the bank. The economics of owning the borrower are superior to the economics of sharing the borrower.

- **Margin structure**: Direct-to-consumer DSCR lenders capture 200–300 bps of total economics (origination fee + SRP + servicing). The white-label model shares economics with the bank, reducing net margin to 100–150 bps. For an established direct lender, this is a margin dilution.

- **Brand investment**: Kiavi, Visio, and Lima One have invested millions in building their brands. White-labeling means their brand is invisible. This contradicts their fundamental go-to-market strategy.

**This is exactly why the white-label channel is available to you.** The incumbents' commitment to the direct model creates a structural opening that they cannot exploit without undermining their core business.

### 10.3 The Infrastructure Moat

Once embedded in a bank's systems, switching costs are enormous:

- **Technology integration**: Even a simple iFrame integration requires IT sign-off, security review, and compliance approval. Replacing it means repeating this process with a new vendor.
- **Loan officer training**: Bank staff are trained on your platform. Re-training on a competitor's system costs time and productivity.
- **Compliance documentation**: The bank's policies and procedures reference your platform. Changing platforms requires updating policies, re-submitting to examiners, and re-training compliance staff.
- **Borrower familiarity**: Borrowers who have used the bank's DSCR portal are familiar with the interface. A change could confuse or alienate them.
- **Servicing continuity**: If you service the loans, switching means transferring servicing — a process that costs $50–$100 per loan and risks borrower attrition.

**Estimated switching cost per bank**: $75,000–$150,000 (hard costs + staff time + risk). At 100 partner banks, a competitor would need to spend $7.5M–$15M just to replace you — with no guarantee of equivalent performance.

### 10.4 The Data Moat

The most powerful and least visible moat is data:

- **Cross-portfolio performance visibility**: You see loan performance across all partner banks. If DSCR 1.25 loans in Ohio are performing worse than expected, you adjust underwriting before any single bank experiences disproportionate losses.
- **Geographic risk intelligence**: You see origination volumes, property types, and performance by MSA. This enables geographic risk management that no individual bank or even a large direct lender can match.
- **Pricing optimization**: With real-time data on secondary market execution across thousands of loans, you can price more precisely than any competitor — capturing margin while remaining competitive.
- **Predictive analytics**: At 5,000+ loans originated, you can build predictive models for prepayment, default, and loss severity that improve underwriting and investor execution.
- **Regulatory intelligence**: Having been reviewed by dozens of examiners across multiple regulatory agencies, you accumulate institutional knowledge about examiner expectations and concerns — a form of regulatory intelligence that no competitor can replicate without the same breadth of partnerships.
